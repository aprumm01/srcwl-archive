# Study Partner Summary: Ethical Encounters in Design Practice

## 1. Overview of the Document

This paper, titled "Ethical Encounters in Design Practice: A Framework Grounded in Practitioner Experience," was authored by Gizem Oz, Christian Dindler, and Peter Gall Krogh from the Department of Digital Design and Information Studies at Aarhus University in Denmark, along with Sharon Lindberg from the Department of Computer and Systems Sciences at Stockholm University in Sweden. The research team brings together expertise in digital design, information studies, and systems sciences, with a particular focus on practice-oriented design ethics research within human-computer interaction.

The document is a peer-reviewed conference paper published in the Proceedings of the 2026 ACM Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI '26), held in Barcelona, Spain from April 13 to 17, 2026. CHI represents the premier international venue for human-computer interaction research, making this publication significant within the field. The paper is licensed under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International License and spans 16 pages, indicating a full research contribution rather than a short paper or work-in-progress.

The significance of this work lies in its contribution to bridging the gap between empirical observations of design ethics and operational frameworks that practitioners can actually use. While substantial empirical research has documented how designers experience ethical issues in practice, the authors identify a critical need for conceptual and framework-building research that provides shared vocabulary and actionable guidance. This paper addresses that gap by presenting an empirically grounded framework that emerged from 98 accounts of ethical encounters shared by 27 design practitioners across three European countries. The framework positions ethics not as an external constraint on design but as an embedded, generative, and evolving part of design work. For the HCI and informatics community, particularly those concerned with sustainability, resilience, and computing within limits, this paper offers valuable insights into how ethical considerations manifest in everyday design practice and how practitioners develop capacities to navigate them over time.

## 2. Research Overview

The central research questions driving this study ask: What are the lived experiences of design practitioners in terms of design ethics in their practice? And how do design practitioners navigate within those situations? The authors seek to understand ethics as it emerges from the lived, situated dynamics of design practice rather than as abstract principles or formal guidelines that often fail to influence day-to-day decision-making.

The methodological approach employs semi-structured interviews with 27 multidisciplinary design practitioners from three European countries: Denmark (16 participants), Italy (7), and Switzerland (4). The sample included 10 females and 17 males, all with mid to senior level experience (3 to 7 years and 8 or more years) in the technology and design sector. Interviews lasted 60 to 90 minutes and were conducted in person with participants from Italy and Switzerland (in English) and in Danish with participants from Denmark (later translated by Danish-speaking authors). Participants were asked to bring computers to show project examples, and interviewers prompted them with questions such as "Can you give a concrete example?" and "Have you ever encountered such an issue in your work?" to elicit specific accounts rather than abstract definitions.

Data analysis proceeded through three rounds of coding. First, selective tagging focused on concrete, firsthand experience accounts, yielding 98 relevant encounters (67 pertaining to UX design practices, 18 to digital service and system design, and 13 to industrial design). Second, descriptive coding unpacked primary dynamics through inductively derived categories including nature of the encounter, actors leading, values at stake, actions taken, phase in design process, outcomes, and professional domain. Third, analytical coding explored relationships between categories to understand how ethical encounters generate new practices and help operationalize design ethics.

A key concept is the definition of ethical encounters themselves. The authors define ethical encounters as "practitioner-identified situations that expose tensions and value-laden decisions, emerging from the situated realities of day-to-day design work" (Oz et al., 2026, p. 1). This definition emphasizes that ethics in practice is not about applying predetermined rules but about recognizing moments that practitioners themselves identify as ethically charged and navigating the tensions that arise within specific contexts and constraints.

## 3. Theories of Knowledge

The paper draws on several theoretical frameworks that position design ethics as situated, dynamic, and practice-based rather than as static rules or formal codes. The primary theoretical grounding comes from Frauenberger and colleagues' "in-action ethics" framework, which treats ethical matters as "dynamic, unpredictable, and closely tied to practice" requiring "continuous attention throughout the research and design process" (as cited in Oz et al., 2026, p. 2). This framework enables practitioners to engage in "reflection-in-action," critically examining judgments, contexts, and ethical positions as dilemmas arise.

Closely related is Shilton's concept of "values levers," which focuses on specific activities within design practice that can "ignite conversations around values and how ethics can materialize through these activities" (as cited in Oz et al., 2026, p. 2). Values levers function as tools to bring values to the foreground during design, making ethical considerations more explicit and actionable in targeted moments.

The authors also engage with Gray, Chivukula, and colleagues' framework of "ethical design complexity," defined as "complex and choreographed arrangements of ethical considerations that practitioners must navigate" (as cited in Oz et al., 2026, p. 3). This framework positions ethics as "an important mediator of design complexity," emerging from the interplay of individual values and beliefs, organizational practices and cultures, and applied ethical frameworks.

Additionally, the paper builds on Lindberg and colleagues' concept of "cultivating ethics," which offers "a view of ethics as co-constructed and changing over time" through deliberately creating environments where ethical values are integrated into design activities and fostering a culture that prioritizes ethical impact (as cited in Oz et al., 2026, p. 3). The Boyd and Shilton framework of "ethical sensitivity," adapted from other professions, breaks down ethical engagement into recognition (noticing an ethical problem), particularization (building understanding of the situation), and judgment (deciding what to do).

## 4. Central Arguments

The paper's central argument is that ethical encounters in design practice should be understood through three interconnected key dimensions: the perceived issues that trigger encounters, the actions practitioners take to navigate them, and the capacities that emerge as a result. The authors contend that these dimensions are "nested yet multidirectionally connected, without implying a fixed or linear cause-effect" relationship (Oz et al., 2026, p. 11). This framing positions ethics not as a burden or constraint but as a generative force within design work.

The first dimension, perceived issues, encompasses six categories: user advocacy (concerns around design's impact on end users), environmental advocacy (ecological sustainability considerations), personal values (designers' individual beliefs and convictions), legal compliance (particularly GDPR and data handling), ways of working (designer autonomy and process integrity), and value alignment (discovering and maintaining shared values across stakeholders and project phases). Perhaps the most tension-laden encounters occur when "clients' requests conflict with designers' sense of responsibility toward users, such as clients wanting to disguise marketing under technical notifications, push GDPR confirmations too quickly, or obscure the implications of consent" (Oz et al., 2026, p. 5).

The second dimension details seven types of actions: designing features that incorporate ethical considerations directly into products, employing design tools and methods such as workshops and user testing, using dialogue tactics to persuade stakeholders, organizing among colleagues for collective action, establishing formal structures at the organizational level, and sometimes taking no action due to constraints. The authors observe that designers often "accept such navigation as 'part of the job,' as Federica noted, contrasting her work with being 'Michelangelo commissioned to paint,' yet still strive to balance autonomy and client demands" (Oz et al., 2026, p. 6).

The third dimension identifies seven capacities that emerge from encounters: new roles (such as advocate, educator, or activist), tools, processes, and skills, new design ideas and principles, filtering mechanisms for ideas and demands, building relationships with stakeholders and experts, organizational processes, and ethos building that fosters shared understanding of ethical practice. The framework suggests that "while ethical encounters can be challenging, they also can generate opportunities for reflection, negotiation, and ethical cultivation within design work" (Oz et al., 2026, p. 10).

A critical sub-claim addresses the temporal nature of ethical encounters across project phases. The authors demonstrate that different issues, actions, and capacities manifest distinctly in pre-project, project initiation, project execution, post-project, and general phases, offering practitioners an analytic lens for anticipating how ethical encounters might evolve throughout a project's lifecycle.

## 5. Evidence

The evidence supporting the framework derives from qualitative analysis of 98 firsthand accounts of ethical encounters collected from 27 design practitioners. The strength of this evidence lies in its grounding in actual practice rather than hypothetical scenarios or abstract reasoning. Participants provided rich, contextualized descriptions of specific moments when ethical considerations arose in their work.

For user advocacy, designers described declining projects with clients such as tobacco or betting companies due to concerns about user health and well-being. Carl's agency declined a project despite finding it "professionally super exciting" because they did not want to "motivate more people to smoke" (Oz et al., 2026, p. 5). Clients themselves sometimes raised user-related issues at project outset, such as a municipality client who emphasized that a chatbot should "not express religious labels either in expression or speech" while remaining "friendly and cozy" for users of diverse backgrounds (Oz et al., 2026, p. 5).

For environmental advocacy, Emma described discomfort working with an industrial agriculture client that conflicted with "animal welfare and ecological thinking" (Oz et al., 2026, p. 5). One interviewee pointed out the economic tensions in sustainability advocacy, noting he would not advocate for "the most sustainable material when it is far too expensive," highlighting how environmental advocacy is negotiated alongside client demands and market realities (Oz et al., 2026, p. 5).

Evidence for actions taken includes Theo's account of modifying a mobile payment app to "prevent small, uncomfortable money requests by setting a minimum transaction limit" (Oz et al., 2026, p. 6). Peter and Anker abandoned tracking functionalities because "users did not want their time spent in a system logged, which made them feel 'watched'" (Oz et al., 2026, p. 6). For dialogue tactics, Esther printed the entire check-in flow of a flight app, which was "so long that it was taller than me," visually demonstrating excessive complexity to stakeholders (Oz et al., 2026, p. 7).

The evidence for emerging capacities includes Peter's account of persuading his bidding manager to withdraw from a project after discovering it involved selling user data to third parties, which "did not sit right with them" (Oz et al., 2026, p. 8). Nora convinced a client developing an investment app to include educational content so users could make informed choices, demonstrating how encounters can reshape products and processes (Oz et al., 2026, p. 9).

Regarding methodological limitations, the authors acknowledge several constraints. First, the sample is geographically limited to three European countries, though some participants worked for multinational agencies. Second, the interview-based method relies on retrospective accounts, which may be "selective and shaped by personal perspective" (Oz et al., 2026, p. 4). Third, participants were recruited only from design consultancies, not from in-house design teams or other organizational contexts. Fourth, the data reflects only designers' perspectives, not those of clients, managers, or other stakeholders involved in ethical encounters. Fifth, discussing sensitive ethical issues may lead interviewees to self-censor due to concerns about "fear of job loss, potential breach of NDAs, and discomfort discussing colleagues, organizations, and clients" (Oz et al., 2026, p. 4). The authors appropriately acknowledge that "further research is needed to gather multiple perspectives from various actors and across different organizations with a stake in the design process to capture the complexity of ethical encounters" (Oz et al., 2026, p. 4).

## 6. Conclusion

This paper makes a significant contribution to practice-oriented design ethics research by introducing an empirically grounded framework for understanding ethical encounters in design practice. Six months from now, the key takeaways to remember are as follows.

The ethical encounters framework proposes three interconnected dimensions: issues, actions, and capacities. Issues include user advocacy, environmental advocacy, personal values, legal compliance, ways of working, and value alignment. Actions range from designing features and employing design tools to using dialogue tactics, organizing among colleagues, establishing formal structures, and sometimes taking no action. Capacities that emerge include new roles, tools and skills, design ideas, filtering mechanisms, relationships, organizational processes, and ethos building. These dimensions are nested but non-linear, with movement possible in multiple directions across dimensions and project phases.

The framework's practical value lies in its ability to function as what Wobbrock and Kientz call a "vehicle of thought," sensitizing practitioners and researchers to different kinds of issues, actions, and capacities while supporting professional judgment rather than prescribing specific solutions. It reframes ethics from a constraint or risk-avoidance activity into something generative that can build practitioner capacity and improve design outcomes.

The temporal dimension is particularly valuable, showing how ethical encounters manifest differently across pre-project (where organizational-level involvement is most visible through client selection and guidelines), project initiation (where work distribution and stakeholder engagement dominate), project execution (where the widest range of issues arise and designers have most autonomy), post-project (where embedded values must be maintained), and general ongoing activities (such as team meetings and training programs).

For the SRCwL seminar context specifically, this paper offers relevant insights about how sustainability and environmental considerations actually surface in design practice. Environmental advocacy emerged as a distinct category of ethical issues, but the evidence suggests it is often negotiated against economic constraints and client demands. The framework provides conceptual tools for understanding why values like sustainability may be difficult to maintain in commercial design contexts and how practitioners develop capacities to advocate for them over time.

The paper also implicitly addresses questions of resilience in design practice by showing how practitioners navigate tensions between their ethical commitments and organizational, economic, and client pressures. The concept of "no-action" as a legitimate category of response acknowledges that practitioners operate within structural constraints that limit their agency, a realistic assessment that avoids both naive optimism and cynical resignation about the possibility of ethical design practice.

## 8. Discussion Questions

1. The framework identifies "no-action" as a legitimate response to ethical encounters, often due to constraints such as fear of job loss, lack of organizational support, or insufficient knowledge. How should we evaluate the ethics of inaction in design practice? Does the framework adequately address the structural conditions that produce inaction, or does it risk normalizing complicity with harmful outcomes by including no-action as a neutral category?

2. Environmental advocacy emerged as one of six categories of ethical issues, but the evidence suggests designers often compromise environmental values when they conflict with economic constraints. Given the urgency of climate change and ecological limits, is the framework's descriptive approach sufficient, or should practice-oriented ethics research take a more normative stance about which values should take priority in design decisions?

3. The participants were all from design consultancies in wealthy European countries, working primarily on UX and digital service design. How might the framework need to be modified or extended to account for ethical encounters in different contexts, such as design work in the Global South, hardware and manufacturing design, or design conducted within large technology corporations with vastly more resources and impact?

4. The framework emphasizes individual practitioner experience and agency, with collective action (organizing among colleagues) appearing as just one of several action types. Given that many ethical challenges in design stem from systemic issues like capitalism, colonialism, or extractive business models, does focusing on individual ethical encounters risk obscuring the need for collective organizing and structural change in the design industry?
