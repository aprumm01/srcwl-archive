# Study Partner Summary: Doing Responsibilities in Entangled Worlds

## 1. Overview of the Document

This peer-reviewed article was authored by Verena Fuchsberger and Christopher Frauenberger, both affiliated with the Human-Computer Interaction Division at the Department for Artificial Intelligence and Human Interfaces, University of Salzburg, Austria. The authors contributed equally to the work and are established scholars in posthumanist HCI research. Fuchsberger is a Postdoc whose research focuses on the agency that humans and nonhuman actors develop together in digital-physical realities, drawing on posthumanist thinking, New Materialism, and Actor-Network Theory. Frauenberger is a full professor who investigates interactive digital technology with marginalized groups and builds philosophical perspectives to conceptualize entangled relationships with technology.

The article was published in Human-Computer Interaction (Volume 40, Numbers 1-4, pages 65-88), a top-tier journal in the field. It was received on March 16, 2023, revised on September 5, 2023, accepted on October 9, 2023, and published online on October 30, 2023. The work was supported by the Austrian Science Fund. The journal publication context places this within a special issue on sustainability, resilience, and computing within limits, making it directly relevant to the I609 course's concerns about how computing intersects with broader planetary and social challenges.

The document represents a theoretical contribution to the discourse on responsibility in technology design and use, situated at the intersection of philosophy, ethics, and human-computer interaction. Its significance lies in its attempt to reconceptualize responsibility away from traditional individualist, human-centered frameworks toward a relational, performative understanding that accounts for the complex entanglements between humans and increasingly autonomous technologies. This reconceptualization responds to what the authors identify as a "responsibility gap" that emerges when technologies gain agential capacities that exceed traditional frameworks of moral attribution. The work synthesizes insights from Actor-Network Theory, agential realism, and postphenomenology to propose that responsibility should be understood not as an attribute possessed by individual agents but as something continuously produced within hybrid assemblages of human and nonhuman actors.

## 2. Research Overview

The central research question driving this article asks how we can conceptualize responsibility in a world where humans may no longer be the only legitimate sources of moral agency. The authors argue that modern computing technology has reached levels of pervasiveness and agential capabilities that make it difficult to argue these systems are not deeply implicated in morally relevant action. Traditional ways of attributing or distributing responsibility to different human actors become untenable in such highly complex, entangled socio-technical contexts.

The methodological approach is primarily theoretical and conceptual rather than empirical. The authors engage in a philosophical investigation, reviewing the origins of responsibility as a concept, examining how it has been addressed in existing HCI and technology ethics literature, and then drawing on posthumanist theories to develop an alternative framework. They support their theoretical arguments with illustrative examples from technology contexts, including Microsoft's chatbot Tay, period tracking apps, fitness trackers, predictive policing systems, and the development of the Internet through ARPANET.

The key concept the authors develop is "doing responsibilities," which represents a shift from understanding responsibility as a static attribute that individuals or collectives possess to viewing it as a performative, relational activity that is continuously enacted within fluid assemblages. As the authors state, this "posthumanist lens toward responsibility leads us to suggest doing responsibilities as a term that differentiates a static understanding of responsibility from a performative one. It emphasizes that responsibilities are fluid, unique, and multiple; that they are performed collectively and inseparably among human and nonhuman actors; that they concern and are produced by humans and nonhumans alike; and that they are not distributed, but shared within hybrid assemblages of actors" (Fuchsberger & Frauenberger, 2025, p. 74). This reconceptualization draws on the posthumanist insight that ontological categories are not pre-given but are continuously produced through material-discursive practices, meaning that responsibility itself emerges through relations rather than residing in pre-figured entities.

## 3. Theories of Knowledge

The article draws on three interconnected posthumanist theoretical frameworks that share commitments to relational ontology and performativity.

Actor-Network Theory (ANT) provides the foundational perspective that "the social" is constituted of relations among actors, both human and nonhuman alike. ANT posits that acting is the minimum requirement for being an actor, and the network that results from acting actors is what makes up the social. The authors draw on Bruno Latour's articulation that what "counts as a person is an effect generated by a network of heterogeneous, interacting, materials" (Law, 1992, as cited in Fuchsberger & Frauenberger, 2025, p. 71). ANT provides a vocabulary for describing actors and their relations without hunting for causes but rather tracing effects where responsibility sits, develops, changes, vanishes, or hides.

Agential realism, developed by Karen Barad, extends this relational thinking by arguing that ontological categories are not static but are continuously produced in a materially discursive and performative manner through "intra-actions." Barad's account collapses the traditional separation between ontology and epistemology into an "ethico-onto-epistemological" perspective where there is "no way of getting away from ethics" because "the delicate tissue of ethicality runs through the marrow of being" (Barad, as cited in Fuchsberger & Frauenberger, 2025, p. 72). This framework locates responsibility in the quality and nature of relationships rather than in pre-figured entities.

Postphenomenology, drawing on Don Ihde's extension of Heidegger's tool analysis, introduces the concept of mediation to describe how technologies shape how we make sense of the world, how we act in it, and the moral frame by which we judge these actions. Building on this, Peter-Paul Verbeek develops an account of moralizing technology that unsettles traditional requirements for moral agency, arguing that technologies gain moral significance by playing explicit and active roles in our moral actions.

## 4. Central Arguments

The central argument of this article is that responsibility must be reconceptualized from an individual attribute to a relational and performative activity that is continuously produced within hybrid assemblages of human and nonhuman actors. The authors contend that traditional philosophical and ethical frameworks for understanding responsibility are inadequate for addressing the moral complexities introduced by emerging technologies with increasing agential capacities.

The authors explicitly reject both extremes of the current debate: they do not want to offer "more than a reductive argument for holding machines morally responsible, with all the problematic implications that this would entail" (Fuchsberger & Frauenberger, 2025, p. 71). Instead, they seek to construct "a nuanced argument for a fluid and performative notion of responsibility that is continuously produced by assemblages of human and nonhuman actors" (Fuchsberger & Frauenberger, 2025, p. 71).

A crucial sub-claim is that the "responsibility gap" cannot be filled by simply distributing appropriate amounts of responsibility to different actors. Through the frame of shared responsibility within hybrid assemblages, "a responsibility gap signals a lack of doing responsibility productively within relations" (Fuchsberger & Frauenberger, 2025, p. 74). This means that assemblages may be misconfigured or that implicated actors engage with the assemblage in irresponsible ways, intentionally or not.

The authors trace how responsibilities are done across different temporal phases of technological innovation. In the "milieu of innovation," responsibility begins before any design process kicks off, shaped by the intellectual, economic, technical, and cultural context within which technological futures are envisioned. During design, responsibility is not just a matter of design but emerges when designers are part of changing assemblages within the broader milieu of innovation. After design, responsibilities continue being done within fluid assemblages of use, through appropriation, negotiation, and relationship-building between human intentionality and technological programs of action.

A politically significant argument is that the predominant narrative of big tech seeks to detach societies from the process of innovation by portraying it as transcendent and inevitable. The authors argue that asking how and why this is done may lead us to productive ways of re-configuring and reengaging with doing responsibilities for alternative future realities.

## 5. Evidence

The authors support their theoretical arguments through a combination of philosophical reasoning, synthesis of existing literature, and illustrative examples from technology contexts. The evidence marshaled spans several categories.

First, they draw extensively on philosophical sources to establish the inadequacy of traditional responsibility frameworks. They trace how moral philosophy has struggled with questions of determinism, forward-looking versus backward-looking accounts, collective responsibility, and the responsibility gap that emerges with increasingly autonomous technologies. They cite Hans Jonas's ethical imperative for the technological age, which speaks to humanity as a whole rather than individual actions, and Christopher List's philosophical analysis of whether AI systems can bear responsibility similar to corporations.

Second, the authors provide illustrative examples that demonstrate how responsibility is done within assemblages. The Microsoft Tay chatbot example shows how responsibility cannot be located in any single actor: the technology was not built with racist intentions but became racist through interacting with people, yet the company was rightly held accountable for creating a system that amplified extremist views. The period tracking app example illustrates how responsibilities were continuously done in ways that brought about harmful realities for women when abortion laws changed, with designers, users, apps, policymakers, and data all entangled in producing consequences that no single actor fully controlled.

Third, they cite Science and Technology Studies research on how innovation milieus shape what technologies become possible. The history of ARPANET demonstrates how public funding, business interests, and technological opportunity spaces created conditions for certain futures while foreclosing others. Shoshana Zuboff's analysis of surveillance capitalism shows how economic modes of monetizing become intimately entangled with what technologies are developed.

The methodological approach has inherent limitations that the authors acknowledge. Because this is primarily a theoretical contribution rather than an empirical study, the illustrative examples serve to demonstrate the plausibility and productivity of the framework rather than to test it systematically. The authors recognize that "putting such more-than-human notions of responsibility into practice still requires many problems to be solved" (Fuchsberger & Frauenberger, 2025, p. 84). They note it is "all too easy to fall victim to unintended consequences when starting to loosen strict requirements on moral agency" and that "it is a fine line to introduce nonhumans into the picture without potentially making them scapegoats for human (ir)responsibilities" (Fuchsberger & Frauenberger, 2025, p. 84).

The authors also acknowledge critiques from scholars like Makela and Hakli who argue that extending moral responsibility to assemblages including AI and social robots might only help humans hiding behind such collectives. The authors believe this concern is warranted but argue that practical and legal remedies need to be found, not ethical ones, because extending the discourse around moral agency and responsibility is necessary to advance how we address technological futures.

## 6. Conclusion

This article makes a significant contribution to sustainability-oriented HCI by offering a theoretical framework that reconceptualizes responsibility as a performative, relational activity rather than a static attribute. For scholars and practitioners working on computing within limits, this reframing has profound implications for how we think about who and what is responsible for the environmental and social impacts of digital technologies.

The core insight to retain is that responsibility is not something to be distributed among pre-figured entities but something that is continuously produced within hybrid assemblages of human and nonhuman actors. This shift from "having responsibility" to "doing responsibilities" has three major implications that the authors identify. Methodologically, considering assemblages as sources for doing responsibility requires researchers to look beyond human actors and identify human-nonhuman actors and their relations that are temporal, spatial, dimensional, and gradual. Theoretically, assemblages need to be described and framed within epistemological units of analysis that include articulation of researchers' own intra-actions, framing not just what to blame or praise but also the assemblages' potential and capacities for inducing desirable change. Designerly, practitioners need to configure the milieus they operate within to produce desired realities, paying attention to sustainability in all endeavors, independent from particular use cases or actors, concerning innovation processes, after design, and even post use.

The framework challenges computing within limits scholarship to think beyond technical efficiency or individual behavior change as paths to sustainability. Instead, it suggests that matters of sustainability require attention to how assemblages are configured, how responsibilities are done within them, and how we might reconfigure relations to produce more just and sustainable futures. The political dimension is crucial: the predominant narrative of technological inevitability serves to disengage societies from innovation processes, but asking how and why responsibilities are done can open productive ways of reconfiguring assemblages.

For exam or discussion recall, remember that this article bridges posthumanist philosophy and HCI ethics to argue that emerging technologies with agential capacities require us to move beyond individualist frameworks of moral responsibility. The concept of "doing responsibilities" captures how responsibility is fluid, multiple, shared (not distributed), and continuously produced through relations within hybrid assemblages. The practical upshot is not to assign responsibility to machines but to pay attention to how responsibilities are enacted across the full lifecycle of technological innovation, from the milieu of origin through design and into use and appropriation.

## 8. Discussion Questions

1. The authors argue that responsibility should be understood as "shared" within assemblages rather than "distributed" among actors. What are the practical implications of this distinction for designing accountability mechanisms in AI systems? Does the concept of shared responsibility risk enabling responsibility avoidance, or does it offer more productive pathways for intervention?

2. The article uses the example of period tracking apps to illustrate how responsibilities are continuously done within fluid assemblages. How might this framework change how HCI researchers approach privacy by design, particularly when legal and political contexts shift in ways designers did not anticipate?

3. The authors identify the "milieu of innovation" as a site where responsibilities are already being done before any formal design process begins. How might computing within limits researchers apply this insight to understand how unsustainable assumptions about growth, data, and energy become embedded in technological futures before individual designers make choices?

4. Fuchsberger and Frauenberger acknowledge that introducing nonhumans into responsibility frameworks risks making them "scapegoats for human (ir)responsibilities." How can researchers and practitioners operationalize the doing responsibilities framework while maintaining meaningful accountability for human actors who hold power within socio-technical assemblages?
