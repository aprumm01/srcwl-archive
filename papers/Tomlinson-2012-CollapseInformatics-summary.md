## 2. Research Overview

**Central Research Question:**
The paper addresses the question: How can the HCI community prepare for and contribute to a potential future of civilizational collapse by designing sociotechnical systems in the present that will remain useful in contexts of scarcity, limited infrastructure, and reduced sociopolitical complexity?

**Who Conducted the Study:**
The research was conducted collaboratively by five researchers across two major American research universities (University of California, Irvine and Indiana University, Bloomington) with additional affiliation at an independent research organization (Bureau of Economic Interpretation). The work was supported by the National Science Foundation under Grant No. 0644415 and by the Alfred P. Sloan Foundation.

**Data and Methods:**
This is a theoretical/conceptual paper rather than an empirical study. The authors employ a literature synthesis methodology, drawing from multiple disciplines including environmental studies, anthropology, archaeology, economics, climate science, and existing HCI sustainability research. They integrate these perspectives to construct a new theoretical framework and research agenda. The paper also includes design sketches and "fictional abstracts" as speculative design exercises to illustrate potential collapse informatics projects.

**Key Concepts Defined:**
- **Collapse:** Drawing from archaeologist Joseph Tainter, collapse is defined as "a rapid, significant loss of an established level of sociopolitical complexity" (Tomlinson et al., 2012, p. 655), where "rapid" means "no more than a few decades" (p. 655).
- **Collapse Informatics:** "the study, design, and development of sociotechnical systems in the abundant present for use in a future of scarcity" (Tomlinson et al., 2012, p. 655-656).
- **Civilization:** "a large, complex society, where a society is a collection of cooperating individuals (or smaller such collections)" (Tomlinson et al., 2012, p. 656).
- **Vulnerability:** Defined following Luers et al. as relating to "(a) the sensitivity of the system's well-being (or its proxy) to changes in the value of the environmental variable in question and (b) the proximity of the system to a threshold below which the system is said to be 'damaged'" (Tomlinson et al., 2012, p. 658).
- **Adaptive Capacity:** "the extent to which a system can modify its circumstances to move to a less vulnerable condition" (Tomlinson et al., 2012, p. 658).

**Direct Verbatim Quote:**
The authors articulate the core rationale for collapse informatics: "We are at a crossroads where the logic that applies is the logic of hoping for the best and preparing for the worst—that is, we need to continue our efforts to develop digital interactivity to induce people and societies to engage in behavioral changes that reduce our greenhouse gas emissions, and we also need to develop digital interactivity that allows us to prepare for and adapt to climate change under the possibility that we will reach a tipping point, or the possibility that we have already done so" (Tomlinson et al., 2012, p. 657).

## 3. Theories of Knowledge

**Key Theoretical Frameworks:**

1. **Tainter's Theory of Collapse:** The paper draws heavily on Joseph Tainter's archaeological and anthropological work on the collapse of complex societies. Tainter argues that collapse manifests as "a lower degree of stratification and social differentiation; less economic and occupational specialization, of individuals, groups, and territories; less centralized control; that is, less regulation and integration of diverse economic and political groups by elites; less behavioral control and regimentation; less investment in the epiphenomena of complexity, those elements that define the concept of 'civilization': monumental architecture, artistic and literary achievements, and the like; less flow of information between individuals, between political and economic groups, and between a center and its periphery; less sharing, trading, and redistribution of resources; less overall coordination and organization of individuals and groups; a smaller territory integrated within a single political unit" (Tomlinson et al., 2012, p. 655).

2. **Max-Neef's Theory of Basic Human Needs:** The authors employ economist Manfred Max-Neef's framework which "argues that the fundamental human needs are subsistence, protection, affection, understanding, participation, idleness, creation, identity, and freedom" (Tomlinson et al., 2012, p. 658). This framework helps identify which needs sociotechnical systems should prioritize in collapse scenarios.

3. **Vulnerability and Adaptive Capacity Framework:** Drawing from Amy Luers et al.'s environmental change research, the authors distinguish between vulnerability (actual current conditions and proximity to damage thresholds) and adaptive capacity (potential to modify circumstances). This framework structures their approach to collapse preparation: "Vulnerability describes actual conditions; adaptive capacity describes possible conditions" (Tomlinson et al., 2012, p. 658).

4. **DiSalvo's Genre Theory of HCI Research:** The paper positions collapse informatics as a new "genre" of HCI research following DiSalvo et al., who describe genres as "common frameworks that structure how researchers define [a problem] and structure what is an appropriate solution" (Tomlinson et al., 2012, p. 657).

5. **Fry's Redirective Practice:** The authors incorporate Tony Fry's notions of "redirective practice" (e.g., using a push mower instead of gas-powered) and "acts of elimination" (e.g., replacing lawns with gardens) as relevant design strategies for reducing complexity.

## 4. Central Arguments

**Central Argument:**
The paper's central argument is that the HCI community must expand beyond its current focus on sustainability and mitigation to include preparation for potential civilizational collapse. The authors contend that designing sociotechnical systems now—while resources and infrastructure are abundant—for use in future contexts of scarcity is both prudent and necessary given the scientific evidence suggesting collapse may be imminent or already underway.

The authors state: "In this paper, we propose that there is a need for research in collapse informatics—the study, design, and development of sociotechnical systems in the abundant present for use in a future of scarcity—as a complement to ICT4D and mitigation-oriented sustainable HCI" (Tomlinson et al., 2012, p. 655-656).

**Key Sub-Claims:**

1. **Collapse is plausible and potentially imminent:** Drawing from multiple sources including the IPCC, Jared Diamond, and Joseph Tainter, the authors argue that "collapse could begin within the next several decades. If it does, it is likely to usher in profound changes" (Tomlinson et al., 2012, p. 656). The IPCC "states that we are very likely to reach a tipping point by 2040 with respect to global warming/climate change unless we are able to reduce our GHG emissions to 1999 levels by the year 2015" (Tomlinson et al., 2012, p. 656).

2. **Collapse is not apocalypse:** The paper distinguishes collapse informatics from apocalyptic scenarios, noting that "collapse frequently occurs more gradually" than the rapid disasters depicted in popular media, potentially taking the form of "an 'N-dip recession'" (Tomlinson et al., 2012, p. 656).

3. **Collapse informatics is a distinct but related genre:** While sharing concerns with sustainable HCI, ICT4D, and crisis informatics, collapse informatics is distinct in its temporal orientation (preparing for future scarcity rather than present abundance), its user base (designing for people who may not yet exist), and its assumptions about infrastructure (anticipating degradation rather than persistence).

4. **ICT is both part of the problem and potential solution:** The authors note that "information technology played a major role in the crisis" of economic collapse (Tomlinson et al., 2012, p. 657), citing Vardi's observation that "if a massive electromagnetic pulse wiped out our computing infrastructure, our society would face a catastrophic collapse" (Tomlinson et al., 2012, p. 657). Yet ICT is also "a force multiplier" that could enable adaptation.

5. **Cross-cultural design challenges are unique:** Collapse informatics "is a cross-cultural design problem" (Tomlinson et al., 2012, p. 659) because designers must create for users who "are not yet in the imagined context of use (collapse). They may not have even been born yet" (Tomlinson et al., 2012, p. 659).

6. **The work has value even if collapse never occurs:** The authors argue that collapse informatics research would benefit ICT4D, disaster preparedness, and localized contexts even "in the event that collapse never occurs" (Tomlinson et al., 2012, p. 655).

## 5. Evidence

**Evidence Supporting Arguments:**

The authors marshal evidence from multiple disciplinary sources to support their arguments:

**Environmental and Climate Science Evidence:**
- The IPCC Climate Change 2007 Synthesis Report indicating a likely tipping point by 2040 unless emissions are drastically reduced
- Jared Diamond's research documenting "numerous examples of collapse across the history of human civilizations" (Tomlinson et al., 2012, p. 656)
- James Lovelock's Gaia Theory predictions about environmental tipping points
- Research on peak oil indicating that "declining returns for our reliance on fossil fuels, and possibly for some raw materials" have been reached (Tomlinson et al., 2012, p. 656)

**Archaeological and Anthropological Evidence:**
- Joseph Tainter's comprehensive study *The Collapse of Complex Societies* documenting historical collapse patterns
- Evidence that "most civilizations that have ever existed have collapsed" (Tomlinson et al., 2012, p. 655)
- Historical analyses showing collapse typically occurs over "no more than a few decades" rather than centuries

**Economic and Technological Evidence:**
- Moshe Vardi's analysis in CACM that "information technology played a major role in the crisis" of economic collapse (Tomlinson et al., 2012, p. 657)
- Tainter's observation that "a common thread to these disasters is that our financial system has reached a level of complexity that makes it opaque even to the 'high priests of finance'" (Tomlinson et al., 2012, p. 657)
- The energy subsidy argument that "one barrel of oil does an equivalent amount of work to 25,000 hours of human manual labor" (Tomlinson et al., 2012, p. 656), making current lifestyles dependent on fossil fuels

**Design Example Evidence:**
The authors provide a detailed example project—the Climate Change Habitability Index (CCHI)—as evidence that collapse informatics research is feasible and valuable. The CCHI would allow individuals to answer questions such as: "(a) can I continue to live where I am living, (b) where can I move if I can't continue to live where I'm living, and (c) how many people can the place where I live sustainably support, if where I live continues to be habitable?" (Tomlinson et al., 2012, p. 660).

**Fictional Abstracts as Speculative Evidence:**
The paper includes several "fictional abstracts" describing potential research projects to illustrate the breadth of collapse informatics:
- Designing ICT systems for post-collapse deployment ("collapse-compliant systems")
- Modeling survivable whole life systems based on Max-Neef's human needs
- Visualizing collapse via markers of social complexity
- Peak ICT research exploring when information availability will decline
- Local smart grids for off-grid communities
- Tracking post-collapse food sources
- Optimizing arbitrage in dynamic barter markets
- Alternative currency and gatekeeping systems

**Methodological Limitations:**

1. **Lack of Empirical Validation:** The paper acknowledges that "evaluation is a difficult and rich topic of critical importance to collapse informatics, and fertile ground for future work in this area" (Tomlinson et al., 2012, p. 660). The authors admit that evaluation of systems for futures that "have not yet come to pass" presents fundamental challenges.

2. **Uncertainty About Collapse Timing and Form:** The authors acknowledge significant uncertainty: "When, exactly, collapse may begin is difficult to predict with precision" and "There is some debate over the timing of this phenomenon; some researchers say it has already occurred, others that it will occur within the next several decades" (Tomlinson et al., 2012, p. 656).

3. **Cross-Cultural Design Limitations:** The paper notes that "it is impossible for a researcher or designer to engage with the anticipated users and seek to understand their needs and ways of viewing the world in which they live" (Tomlinson et al., 2012, p. 659) because those users may not yet exist.

4. **Western/Developed-World Perspective:** The Bariloche Group's critique is cited: "The view that global crises will occur in the future reflects a parochial, developed-world perspective. For two-thirds of the world's population, crises of scarce resources, inadequate housing, deplorable conditions of health, and starvation are already at hand" (Tomlinson et al., 2012, p. 663).

5. **Theoretical Rather Than Empirical:** The paper is primarily a position paper establishing a research agenda rather than reporting empirical findings, limiting its ability to demonstrate the practical value of collapse informatics interventions.

## 6. Conclusion

**Synthesis for Long-Term Recall:**

Tomlinson et al.'s 2012 paper introduces "collapse informatics" as a new research genre within HCI, arguing that the computing community must begin designing sociotechnical systems now—while we have abundant resources—for potential use in futures characterized by scarcity, infrastructure degradation, and reduced sociopolitical complexity.

**The Core Problem:** Global industrial civilization may be approaching collapse due to climate change, peak oil, and systemic complexity. Historical evidence shows most civilizations eventually collapse, typically within decades once decline begins. The authors argue this is not apocalyptic fantasy but rather prudent preparation based on scientific consensus (IPCC projections, environmental research) and historical patterns (Tainter's archaeological work, Diamond's comparative studies).

**The Proposed Solution:** Collapse informatics—designing ICT systems during abundance for use during scarcity. This differs from:
- *Sustainable HCI* (which focuses on mitigation and assumes continued infrastructure)
- *ICT4D* (which works with present-day developing contexts, not future collapsed ones)
- *Crisis informatics* (which assumes disasters are bounded in time and space)

**Key Analytical Frameworks to Remember:**
1. **Vulnerability vs. Adaptive Capacity:** Vulnerability is current proximity to damage thresholds; adaptive capacity is the ability to change circumstances. Collapse informatics seeks to increase adaptive capacity and reduce vulnerability.
2. **Max-Neef's Human Needs:** Subsistence, protection, affection, understanding, participation, idleness, creation, identity, freedom—these should guide what collapse-era systems prioritize.
3. **Cross-Cultural Design Challenge:** Designing for users who don't yet exist and contexts that haven't materialized is a unique methodological problem.

**Practical Implications:**
- "Collapse-compliant systems" should be: locally cached, critically useful for basic human needs, and robust (containing source code for repair)
- The Climate Change Habitability Index (CCHI) example demonstrates making climate data accessible to ordinary people for migration and adaptation decisions
- Research areas include: post-collapse ICT deployment, smart grids for off-grid communities, barter market optimization, alternative currencies, and collapse monitoring systems

**The "What If Collapse Never Comes?" Argument:** Even if collapse is averted, this research benefits ICT4D, disaster preparedness, and localized community resilience. The authors frame this as "hoping for the best and preparing for the worst."

**Critical Perspective:** The paper has been critiqued (within its own text) for potentially reflecting a Western/developed-world bias, as billions already live in conditions resembling collapse. The evaluation challenge—how do you test systems for futures that haven't arrived?—remains unresolved.

**Why This Matters for HCI:** The paper challenges fundamental assumptions embedded in most technology design: that infrastructure will persist, that complexity is desirable, and that growth is possible indefinitely. It asks HCI researchers to consider their discipline's potential contribution to civilizational resilience rather than merely user convenience or market growth.

## 8. Discussion Questions

1. **Ethical Priorities in Design:** The authors suggest designing for future users who may not yet exist. What ethical frameworks should guide decisions about whose potential needs to prioritize when designing collapse-ready systems? How do we balance the needs of present users against hypothetical future users, especially given that the billions currently living in poverty-like conditions could benefit from similar interventions today?

2. **Technology Dependency Paradox:** The paper notes that ICT both contributed to systemic complexity that hastens collapse and offers tools for adaptation. Given Vardi's warning that "if a massive electromagnetic pulse wiped out our computing infrastructure, our society would face a catastrophic collapse," should collapse informatics focus more on low-tech or no-tech solutions rather than sophisticated digital systems? Can we design ICT systems that reduce overall technological dependency?

3. **Evaluation and Validation:** The authors acknowledge the fundamental challenge of evaluating systems designed for futures that have not yet occurred. What research methodologies from other fields (e.g., speculative design, futures studies, historical analysis) might be adapted to validate collapse informatics interventions? How might the field avoid the trap of unfalsifiable claims?

4. **Political and Economic Implications:** Collapse informatics implicitly challenges the growth-oriented economic assumptions underlying most technology development and venture capital investment. How might this research agenda be funded and institutionally supported given that its premises contradict the interests of many powerful stakeholders? What role should HCI researchers play in broader political discussions about collapse preparation versus collapse prevention?

## 9. Bias Check

**Reflection on Neutrality:**

The paper exhibits several potential biases that readers should consider:

1. **Disciplinary Bias:** As HCI researchers, the authors naturally frame collapse preparation in terms of technological solutions, potentially underemphasizing social, political, or low-tech approaches to resilience. The paper does acknowledge Fry's "acts of elimination" but focuses primarily on building new systems rather than dismantling existing ones.

2. **Geographic/Cultural Bias:** The authors cite the Bariloche Group's critique acknowledging that their perspective may be "parochial" and "developed-world." The paper's examples (Google Earth integration, smart grids, digital currencies) assume baseline access to computing infrastructure that much of the world lacks.

3. **Temporal Urgency Framing:** While citing scientific sources, the paper presents collapse as more imminent and certain than some interpretations of the evidence would support. This framing may serve rhetorical purposes to motivate the research agenda.

4. **Selection Bias in Sources:** The authors draw heavily from collapse-oriented theorists (Tainter, Diamond, Lovelock) while giving less attention to critiques of collapse narratives or evidence of civilizational resilience.

5. **Neutrality Strengths:** The paper does acknowledge uncertainty about timing and form of collapse, presents the "what if collapse never comes?" counterargument, includes the Bariloche Group critique, and positions the work as complementary to rather than replacing existing approaches.

6. **Self-Awareness:** The authors explicitly note that portions of the document were "adapted from several non-archival publications" (p. 655), indicating transparency about the paper's origins.

**Accuracy Score: 7/10**

The paper accurately represents the sources it cites and provides a logically coherent argument supported by credible scientific and scholarly references. However, the theoretical/speculative nature of the work, combined with the inherent uncertainty about future collapse scenarios, limits the extent to which claims can be verified. The paper is more accurately characterized as a well-argued position paper establishing a research agenda than as an empirically validated contribution. The score reflects strong scholarly rigor within its genre constraints, tempered by the acknowledged evaluation challenges and potential framing biases.
