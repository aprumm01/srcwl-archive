# Study Partner Summary: Responsible Stakeholder Engagement in Design Education

## 1. Overview of the Document

The article carries significant weight within the intersecting fields of design education, business ethics, and human-computer interaction.

The authorship team comprises seven researchers spanning multiple institutions and disciplinary backgrounds. Bo T. A cognitive psychologist by training, Christensen's work centers on cognitive studies of creative, innovative, and entrepreneurial practices across design, business, architecture, and engineering. Kasper Merling Arendt, a researcher at the Danish Center for Social Science Research with a PhD in Creativity and Innovation, specializes in survey design and quantitative methods. This document holds particular significance for the field because it provides the first empirical mapping of how design programs worldwide actually teach responsible stakeholder engagement, moving beyond normative prescriptions to descriptive reality. The study bridges management scholarship (stakeholder theory) with design education scholarship, creating a novel theoretical framework that can inform curriculum development across diverse program types.

## 2. Research Overview

The central research question guiding this study is explicitly stated: "What types of stakeholders do design education programs focus on in their teaching, and how does this stakeholder focus impact upon the responsible stakeholder engagement competences taught in the program?" (Christensen et al., 2026, p. 125). This question addresses a significant gap in the literature: while design education increasingly recognizes the importance of social responsibility, there has been limited empirical understanding of what programs actually teach and which stakeholder groups they prioritize.

The researchers employed a quantitative survey methodology, collecting data from June 2024 to March 2025 through a network-based sampling strategy. They recruited participants from three primary sources: the Cumulus Association's member institutions, institutional affiliations of authors published in the Design Research Society's Digital Library (2002-2022), and mapping of Nordic design programs. The target population was program directors, coordinators, and managers of higher-education design programs worldwide. Of 174 completed surveys, 167 program-level responses remained after excluding seven for quality and eligibility issues. Participants averaged 5.7 years of experience in their roles, with 60% serving as program directors.

The study developed self-report measures grounded in stakeholder theory to assess: (1) Responsible Stakeholder Engagement Competences (RSEC), a 12-item higher-order construct with three subdimensions; and (2) Teaching Focus on four stakeholder groups. The RSEC scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency (alpha = 0.91). The researchers employed Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) to test associations between stakeholder teaching focus and RSEC outcomes while controlling for institutional and program-level factors.

Key concepts include Responsible Stakeholder Engagement Competences (RSEC), defined as "the individual knowledge, skills, attitudes, and ethical sensitivities required to effectively identify, understand, interact with, and manage relationships with stakeholders in a way that is transparent, inclusive, ethical, and conducive to sustainable outcomes" (Christensen et al., 2026, p. 123). The distinction between stakeholder work (analytical tools for mapping and categorizing) and stakeholder engagement (interacting with stakeholders through participation, co-creation, and negotiation) proves fundamental to understanding the competencies designers need.

## 3. Theories of Knowledge

The theoretical foundation of this study rests primarily on stakeholder theory, a framework originally developed in business ethics and strategic management. Stakeholder theory, as articulated by R. Edward Freeman in 1984 and refined through subsequent scholarship, challenges the traditional shareholder-centric view of organizations. Freeman defines stakeholders as "any group or individual who can affect or is affected by the achievement of the organization's objectives" (Freeman, 1984, as cited in Christensen et al., 2026, p. 122). The theory emphasizes that businesses should understand themselves as a set of relationships among groups with stakes in organizational activities, including customers, users, employees, suppliers, investors, and communities.

The integration thesis represents a crucial epistemological commitment within stakeholder theory. This thesis posits that most business decisions inherently contain ethical elements, while most ethical decisions implicitly involve business considerations. This perspective challenges the "separation fallacy," which assumes business decisions can be devoid of moral content and ethical decisions can lack business relevance (Harris & Freeman, 2008; Parmar et al., 2010). By rejecting the artificial division between financial and social concerns, stakeholder theory advocates for a holistic approach to organizational decision-making where competing interests must be balanced.

The authors operationalize stakeholder theory through a framework distinguishing primary from secondary stakeholders along production/consumption dimensions. Primary stakeholders are those whose support is indispensable for organizational survival, holding formal claims and owing special duties by management. Secondary stakeholders lack formal claims but may still be affected by organizational actions, thus warranting moral obligations such as avoiding harm and considering unintended consequences. This differentiation establishes varying levels of responsibility toward different stakeholder groups.

The study also draws upon design scholarship traditions including participatory design, value sensitive design, and design justice, recognizing that these traditions emphasize responsibility from different normative standpoints. The epistemological contribution lies in demonstrating how stakeholder theory from management scholarship can coherently integrate these fragmented design traditions under a unified framework for understanding responsibility.

## 4. Central Arguments

The paper advances several interconnected arguments that together constitute a significant contribution to design education scholarship. The overarching thesis contends that design education programs should deliberately prioritize organizational stakeholder engagement to build students' Responsible Stakeholder Engagement Competences. The authors argue that teaching focused on inter- and intraorganizational stakeholders (suppliers, partners, employees, internal colleagues) is the strongest predictor of RSEC development, more so than traditional user-centric or even sustainability-focused teaching.

The first major claim establishes that responsible stakeholder engagement constitutes a multidimensional competence construct. The authors identify three critical trade-offs grounded in stakeholder theory that translate into distinct design competences. "The first trade-off is what we term 'balancing perspectives.' This reflects the designer's need to understand and integrate multiple and often conflicting stakeholder perspectives" (Christensen et al., 2026, p. 123). Programs must train designers in empathy, perspective-taking, and reflective equilibrium to navigate these tensions.

The second dimension involves managing resources responsibly, recognizing "that responsible stakeholder engagement is economically demanding, thus requiring designers to balance moral imperatives with resource constraints" (Christensen et al., 2026, p. 124). Stakeholder theory explicitly connects ethics with organizing: decisions about time, budget, and attention are simultaneously economic and moral, determining how benefits and harms distribute across stakeholders.

The third dimension, negotiating power relations, stems from designers' limited formal powers, "necessitating skilled negotiation and stakeholder dialogue" (Christensen et al., 2026, p. 124). Designers must develop competences in persuasive communication, facilitation, and collaborative problem-solving because stakeholders differ in salience based on power, legitimacy, and urgency.

A crucial sub-claim challenges the sufficiency of user-centric teaching. The authors contend that while teaching focused on users and clients shows the highest mean emphasis across programs (M = 3.88), this focus does not significantly predict higher RSEC. "The null-effect for teaching focusing on users and clients for responsible competences can be read as a success story: when almost all programs already teach user engagement, its presence no longer distinguishes programs on RSEC" (Christensen et al., 2026, p. 133). User-centric approaches have reached saturation, creating a ceiling effect where additional emphasis yields no marginal gains in competence development.

The most provocative finding is that "teaching focused on inter- and intraorganizational stakeholders is the most robust predictor of an educational program's teaching for competences in responsible stakeholder engagement in practice (e.g., beta = 0.42, p < .001 in full SEM)" (Christensen et al., 2026, p. 132). This challenges programs to expand their curricula beyond consumption-side stakeholders to include organizational actors who shape how design actually gets implemented.

## 5. Evidence

The empirical evidence supporting these claims derives from a global survey of 167 design education programs, analyzed through both descriptive statistics and inferential modeling. The sample included programs across diverse institutional contexts: architecture/engineering (36%), digital/graphical/communication design (36%), fashion/arts (8%), and broad multidisciplinary programs (19%). Geographically, 83% of participating institutions were in the Global North, with 22% from Nordic countries specifically.

The descriptive findings reveal systematic patterns in stakeholder teaching emphasis. Programs reported their highest Teaching Focus on Users and Clients (M = 3.88), followed by Social Groups and Cultural Stakeholders (M = 3.39), Design for the Planet (M = 3.27), and lowest for Inter- and Intraorganizational Stakeholders (M = 2.66). These differences proved statistically significant, indicating that organizational stakeholders receive notably less attention despite their strong association with RSEC outcomes.

The structural equation modeling results provide the study's most compelling evidence. The full model demonstrated acceptable fit (CFI = 0.898, TLI = 0.885, RMSEA = 0.068). Teaching Focus on Inter- and Intraorganizational Stakeholders emerged as the only significant predictor of overall RSEC (beta = 0.42, p < .001). Teaching focused on social groups and cultural stakeholders showed marginal association (beta = 0.19, p = .093), while teaching directed toward users and clients (beta = 0.17, p = .179) and design for the planet (beta = -0.13, p = .200) were not significantly associated with RSEC.

Post-hoc OLS regressions predicting each RSEC subdimension reinforced these patterns. For Managing Resources Responsibly, only inter- and intraorganizational stakeholder focus significantly predicted the outcome (beta = 0.36, p = .003). For Balancing Perspectives, both inter- and intraorganizational focus (beta = 0.50, p = .009) and social groups/cultural stakeholders (beta = 0.20, p = .048) emerged as significant predictors after controlling for institutional variables. For Negotiating Power Relations, inter- and intraorganizational focus again proved significant (beta = 0.26, p = .004), with master's-level programs scoring higher than bachelor's programs (beta = 0.31, p = .023).

Institutional differences also emerged. Programs at broad, multidisciplinary universities reported higher overall RSEC (r = 0.20, p < .05), particularly on Balancing Perspectives. Architecture and engineering programs reported significantly lower teaching focus on three of four stakeholder groups. Fashion/Arts programs showed lower Teaching Focus on users and clients but not other groups. These patterns suggest that program type shapes both stakeholder emphasis and competence development.

The study employed multiple validity checks. Ecological validity was assessed through external coding of 25 program webpages, yielding significant correlations between self-reported survey responses and webpage content for Users and Clients (r = 0.53, p = .007) and Inter- or Intraorganizational Stakeholders (r = 0.41, p = .042). Concurrent validity was established through moderate correlations between teaching content and self-reported stakeholder engagement across all four categories (r = 0.45 to 0.50, all p < .001).

The authors acknowledge several methodological limitations that constrain interpretation. The reliance on self-reported data from program leads may reflect idealized rather than actual teaching practices, as directors may not have full visibility into all pedagogical activities. The network-based sampling strategy, while necessary given the absence of a global registry of design programs, means findings should be interpreted as analytic generalizations rather than population estimates. The Global North skew (83%) limits generalizability to programs in the Global South. The cross-sectional design prevents causal claims; the observed associations might reflect selection effects or omitted variables. Finally, key concepts like "responsibility" and "stakeholder engagement" may be interpreted differently across institutional, disciplinary, and cultural contexts, potentially introducing measurement variance.

## 6. Conclusion

This study makes a timely and substantive contribution to understanding how design education can better prepare students for the ethical complexities of contemporary professional practice. By applying stakeholder theory from business ethics to design education, Christensen and colleagues have created a coherent framework that integrates fragmented traditions of participatory design, value sensitive design, and design justice under a unified conceptual umbrella. The Responsible Stakeholder Engagement Competences (RSEC) construct, with its three dimensions of Balancing Perspectives, Negotiating Power Relations, and Managing Resources Responsibly, offers curriculum developers a concrete scaffold for sequencing competence development across degree programs.

The most striking finding for recall six months hence is the paradox at the heart of design education: programs invest heavily in user-centric teaching, yet this emphasis no longer differentiates programs on responsible competence outcomes. The saturation of user-centered approaches creates a ceiling effect where marginal investments yield diminishing returns. Meanwhile, organizational stakeholders (suppliers, implementation partners, internal colleagues, procurement officers) receive the least teaching attention but show the strongest association with RSEC development. This suggests a fundamental rebalancing is needed: programs should not abandon user-centric methods, but must deliberately add organizational literacy to help students understand the governance structures, budget constraints, and power asymmetries within which design decisions actually unfold.

For scholars in sustainability, resilience, and computing within limits, this paper offers a crucial insight: teaching about environmental impacts and planetary boundaries (design for the planet) did not significantly predict RSEC. The authors interpret this finding by noting that sustainability courses often emphasize material properties, life-cycle assessment, and ecological footprint analysis rather than the stakeholder engagement, conflict mediation, and trade-off negotiation that RSEC measures. This suggests that even well-intentioned sustainability curricula may train students in technical analysis without developing the relational competencies needed to navigate the messy politics of implementing sustainable solutions within organizational constraints.

The practical implication for curriculum design is a three-stage developmental sequence. Early coursework should build Balancing Perspectives through user and community engagements that develop empathy and interpretive inquiry. Late-stage experiences should then place students in high-stakes contexts involving Negotiating Power Relations within governance, budget, and compliance constraints. Finally, capstone or internship experiences should embed Managing Resources Responsibly by requiring explicit trade-off reasoning about which stakeholder claims were prioritized and why, with rubrics that evaluate the justification of decisions.

For PhD students in HCI and informatics, this study models rigorous quantitative methodology applied to design education research. The use of structural equation modeling with appropriate fit indices, the triangulation through ecological and concurrent validity checks, and the transparent reporting of effect sizes alongside significance tests exemplify standards the field should embrace. The authors' acknowledgment of generative AI use (ChatGPT 4.0 for conceptual inspiration and minor editing) also demonstrates emerging norms for disclosure.

## 8. Discussion Questions

**Question 1: Organizational Literacy vs. User Advocacy**
The study finds that teaching focused on organizational stakeholders (suppliers, internal colleagues, implementation partners) most strongly predicts responsible stakeholder engagement competences, while user-centric teaching shows no significant effect. Does this finding suggest that design education has overcorrected toward user advocacy at the expense of organizational realism? How might programs balance the emancipatory goals of participatory design with the pragmatic constraints that organizational stakeholders represent, without capitulating to corporate interests that may conflict with user welfare?

**Question 2: Sustainability Teaching and Stakeholder Engagement**
Teaching focused on "design for the planet" (future generations, environmental limits, other species) did not significantly predict RSEC outcomes. The authors suggest this may be because sustainability courses emphasize technical analysis (life-cycle assessment, material impacts) rather than stakeholder engagement skills. For scholars working in computing within limits, what pedagogical innovations might better integrate planetary boundaries thinking with the relational competencies of stakeholder negotiation and trade-off articulation?

**Question 3: Global North Skew and Transferability**
With 83% of participating programs located in the Global North, how might the findings differ if the sample better represented design education in the Global South? Given that stakeholder theory emerged from Western business ethics traditions, what alternative frameworks from non-Western contexts might offer different conceptualizations of responsibility, community, and collective obligation that could enrich or challenge the RSEC construct?

**Question 4: Secondary Stakeholders and Design Justice**
The stakeholder theory framework distinguishes primary stakeholders (with formal claims on organizations) from secondary stakeholders (affected parties without formal claims, including marginalized communities and future generations). How does this distinction interact with design justice frameworks that explicitly center historically marginalized groups as co-producers rather than passive recipients? Could the primary/secondary distinction inadvertently reinforce existing power asymmetries by positioning community members as secondary to paying clients?

## 9. Bias Check

This summary has endeavored to represent the paper's arguments and evidence faithfully, but several potential biases warrant acknowledgment.

**Confirmation bias**: As a study that validates the importance of expanding design education beyond user-centric approaches, there may be temptation to overemphasize findings that align with emerging critiques of human-centered design. I have attempted to balance this by clearly noting the non-significant findings (user/client teaching, planetary teaching) and the authors' interpretations of these null results.

**Disciplinary framing**: The summary adopts the authors' framing of stakeholder theory as a coherent integrating framework. Scholars from participatory design or design justice traditions might contest whether their commitments can be adequately captured within a framework originating from business ethics scholarship. The summary notes but does not deeply interrogate this tension.

**Methodological presentation**: Quantitative findings are presented with their effect sizes and significance levels as reported. The summary accurately conveys the limitations section, including the self-report nature of data, Global North skew, and cross-sectional design that prevents causal inference.

**Author affiliations**: The presence of Colin Gray from Indiana University Bloomington on the author team is noted in the overview, which is relevant context for I609 students but does not bias the substantive summary of findings.
