**Affiliations:**

**Background and Expertise:** Elinor Ostrom (1933-2012) was one of the most influential political economists of the 20th and 21st centuries. She was awarded the Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences in 2009 for her analysis of economic governance, especially the commons. Her groundbreaking work challenged conventional assumptions about the "tragedy of the commons" and demonstrated that local communities can successfully manage shared resources without privatization or top-down government regulation. Her expertise spans political science, public policy, institutional economics, and environmental governance. The Workshop in Political Theory and Policy Analysis at Indiana University, which she co-founded with her husband Vincent Ostrom, has been a leading center for research on collective action and governance for over half a century.

**Type of Document:** This is a peer-reviewed academic article published in the journal *Global Environmental Change* as part of its 20th anniversary issue. The article was received on May 10, 2010, revised on July 8, 2010, and accepted on July 9, 2010. It appears in Volume 20 (2010), pages 550-557.

**Field and Significance:** This article falls within the interdisciplinary fields of environmental governance, political economy, public policy, and climate change studies. Its significance is substantial for several reasons. First, it was published in the year following Ostrom's Nobel Prize, representing a capstone application of her life's work to perhaps the most pressing global challenge of our time: climate change. Second, it directly challenges the dominant policy paradigm that climate change can only be addressed through a single, binding global treaty. Third, it provides both theoretical justification and empirical evidence for why multi-level, polycentric governance approaches may be more effective than monocentric solutions. The article has become a foundational text in discussions of climate governance and continues to influence how scholars and policymakers conceptualize responses to global environmental problems.

## 2. Research Overview

**Central Research Question:** The central question driving this article is whether conventional collective action theory provides the best framework for analyzing how to reduce the threats of climate change, or whether a polycentric approach would improve the analysis of climate policy over exclusive reliance on proposing global solutions. Ostrom asks whether actions taken at less than global scales can cumulatively reduce the threat of major climate change, and examines whether multiple governments and organizations working at multiple scales generate perverse outcomes such as leakage, inconsistent policies, inadequate certification, gaming the system, and free riding.

**Who Conducted the Study:** This is a theoretical and synthetic work authored solely by Elinor Ostrom, drawing on decades of empirical research conducted by herself and colleagues at the Workshop in Political Theory and Policy Analysis at Indiana University. The article synthesizes findings from numerous studies of polycentric governance systems, including research on metropolitan governance, water resource management, and police services.

**Data and Methods:** The methodology is primarily theoretical synthesis combined with review of empirical evidence from multiple sources. Ostrom draws on: (1) in-depth case studies; (2) meta-analyses of cases; (3) large-scale comparative field studies; (4) laboratory experiments; and (5) agent-based models. The article reviews evidence from studies of water industry performance in California (1960s), police services in metropolitan areas, European emissions trading schemes, state-level climate initiatives in the United States, and various local-level efforts to reduce greenhouse gas emissions.

**Key Concepts Defined:**
- **Collective Action:** Settings where decisions about costly actions are made independently but outcomes jointly affect everyone
- **Polycentric Systems:** Governance systems characterized by multiple governing authorities at differing scales rather than a single monocentric unit
- **Free Riding:** When individuals or organizations benefit from collective efforts without contributing an appropriate share

**Direct Verbatim Quote with APA Citation:**
Ostrom provides a foundational definition of polycentricity: "'Polycentric' connotes many centers of decision making that are formally independent of each other.... To the extent that they take each other into account in competitive relationships, enter into various contractual and cooperative undertakings or have recourse to central mechanisms to resolve conflicts, the various political jurisdictions in a metropolitan area may function in a coherent manner with consistent and predictable patterns of interacting behavior. To the extent that this is so, they may be said to function as a 'system'" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 552, citing Ostrom et al., 1961, pp. 831-32).

## 3. Theories of Knowledge

**Key Theoretical Frameworks:**

**1. Conventional Collective Action Theory:** This theory predicts that in situations where decisions about costly actions are made independently but outcomes jointly affect everyone, participants will not achieve feasible outcomes that yield higher returns for all. As Ostrom explains, "Without *externally imposed* regulations at the global scale, the conventional theory predicts that no one will reduce emissions" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 551). The theory assumes that "an external authority determines appropriate actions to be taken, monitors behavior, and imposes sanctions" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 550).

**2. Behavioral Theory of Human Action:** Ostrom advocates for updating collective action theory based on "a behavioral theory of human action and a recognition of the importance of context in affecting levels of trust and reciprocity of those involved" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 551). This theory "assumes that individuals do not posses perfect information but are capable of learning as they interact in a particular setting. Individuals are boundedly rational and do seek benefits for self but vary in their other-regarding preferences and norms about the appropriate actions they should take in particular settings" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 551).

**3. Polycentric Governance Theory:** Originally developed to analyze metropolitan governance, this framework emphasizes multiple, overlapping decision-making centers operating at different scales. Each unit "exercises considerable independence to make norms and rules within a specific domain (such as a family, a firm, a local government, a network of local governments, a state or province, a region, a national government, or an international regime)" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 552).

**Terminology Definitions:**

- **Leakage:** "Two types of leakage can occur from policies adopted at less than global scale: leakage between locations and market leakage" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 554). Location leakage occurs when emissions-reducing activity in one location shifts production to another location; market leakage refers to price structure changes from restrictions on harvesting.

- **Gaming the System:** Strategies that actors adopt to "take advantage of current carbon credit trading systems, including the CDM, that include credits for 'reducing' emissions of six Kyoto Protocol gases" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 555).

- **Inadequate Certification:** The emergence of "global consultants" who may not "possess the requisite knowledge needed to approve projects, were overworked, did not follow instructions, and spent only a few hours evaluating each case" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 555, citing Sovacool and Brown, 2009).

## 4. Central Arguments

**Central Argument:**

Ostrom's central argument is that instead of focusing exclusively on achieving a single global treaty to address climate change, it is more effective to encourage polycentric efforts at multiple scales. She states directly: "I argue that instead of focusing *only* on global efforts (which are indeed a necessary part of the long-term solution), it is better to encourage polycentric efforts to reduce the risks associated with the emission of greenhouse gases. Polycentric approaches facilitate achieving benefits at multiple scales as well as experimentation and learning from experience with diverse policies" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 550).

This argument rests on several foundational claims about the nature of both the climate problem and effective governance:

**Sub-claim 1: Conventional collective action theory has weak empirical support for environmental problems.**
Ostrom argues that "while many instances of free riding are observed in the array of empirical research, a surprisingly large number of small- to medium-scale groups facing collective-action problems do cooperate" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 551). Empirical research has "successfully challenge[d] the conventional theory prediction that those directly affected are helpless and cannot themselves take actions that address a problem of collective action" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 555).

**Sub-claim 2: Benefits from reducing GHG emissions occur at multiple scales, not just globally.**
Ostrom contends that "GHG emissions are the result of an extraordinarily large number of actions taken at multiple scales" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 552). She notes that "communities that have established power networks that enable households to invest in solar power to be used for household energy needs and, when not needed, contributed to a larger power network can reduce local energy costs and GHG emissions" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 552).

**Sub-claim 3: Trust and reciprocity are essential for effective collective action.**
"The capability of those involved to gain a reputation for being trustworthy and reciprocate others' efforts to cooperate with their own cooperation turns out to be a central characteristic of settings where moderate to high levels of cooperation are sustained" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 551). Smaller-scale units facilitate face-to-face discussion and the achievement of common understanding.

**Sub-claim 4: Polycentric systems enable experimentation and learning.**
"Polycentric systems tend to enhance innovation, learning, adaptation, trustworthiness, levels of cooperation of participants, and the achievement of more effective, equitable, and sustainable outcomes at multiple scales" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 552). No governance system is perfect, but polycentric systems have "considerable advantages given their mechanisms for mutual monitoring, learning, and adaptation of better strategies over time" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 552).

## 5. Evidence

**Evidence Supporting Arguments:**

**Empirical Studies of Metropolitan Governance:**
Ostrom draws on decades of research on polycentric governance in U.S. metropolitan areas. Studies of water industry performance in California during the 1960s found that "multiple public and private agencies had searched out productive ways of organizing water resources at multiple scales and simply the presence of multiple government units without a clear hierarchy was not chaotic" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 552). Research on police services found that "while many police departments served the 80 metropolitan areas included in our next study, duplication of specific services by more than one department to the same set of citizens rarely occurred" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 552). Critically, they found that "the 'most efficient producers supply more output for given inputs in high multiplicity metropolitan areas than do the efficient producers in metropolitan areas with fewer producers'" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 552, citing Ostrom & Parks, 1999).

**Local and State Climate Initiatives:**
The article documents extensive local and state-level climate action in the United States:
- "In the United States alone, the mayors of 1026 cities have now joined the U.S. Conference of Mayors' Climate Protection Agreement to reduce GHG emissions by at least 5% relative to 1990 levels" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 553).
- California passed the "Global Warming Solutions Act" in 2006, "aiming for a 25% reduction in GHG emissions by 2020 through requiring drastic reductions from major industries" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 554).
- The Regional Greenhouse Gas Initiative (RGGI), "joined by 10 states in the Northeast and Mid-Atlantic, plans to reduce CO2 emissions from the power sector by 10% by 2018" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 553).

**European Emissions Trading:**
"The performance of the EU Emissions Trading Scheme, for example, has led to substantial reduction of emissions within the EU. The official data issued by the European Environmental Agency (EEA) in 2006 show that the EU members who had signed the Kyoto Agreement were able to achieve a 2% cut in CO2 emissions in 2005 compared to 1990 levels" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 554).

**Household and Community Actions:**
Dietz et al. (2009) "identified 17 actions that can be taken within a home or a business facility that can cumulatively have a major impact on carbon emissions" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 553). Research by Petersen et al. (2007) "found that 'the introduction of feedback, education and incentives resulted in a 32 percent reduction in electricity use'" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 553).

**Methodological Limitations:**

Ostrom acknowledges several limitations and potential problems with polycentric approaches:

1. **Leakage:** "The EU-ETS and RGGI efforts to reduce emissions from industrial producers may, in some cases, simply shift the emissions that would have been produced in Europe or the United States to a developing country that has less stringent policies on GGH emissions or none at all" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 554).

2. **Inconsistent Policies:** "Industrial firms that are trying to develop new technologies to reduce GHGs may find it costly when policies vary in different regions. Potential sales of new technology are limited to those areas where the technology fits the policies adopted" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 555).

3. **Inadequate Certification:** Sovacool and Brown (2009) "evaluated 93 randomly chosen CDM projects and 'found that in a majority of cases the consultants hired to validate CERs [Certified Emission Reports] did not possess the requisite knowledge needed to approve projects, were overworked, did not follow instructions, and spent only a few hours evaluating each case'" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 555).

4. **Gaming the System:** "Wara (2007) examined the distribution of CDM projects and noted that only one-third of emission reductions involve lowering CO2 or reducing energy production. Instead, 'the largest volume of credits, almost 30% of the entire market, come from capturing and destroying trifluoromethane (HFC-23)'" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 555).

5. **Free Riding:** "At the current time, there are many government and private entities at multiple scales that are increasing their GHG emissions substantially--especially in the developing world--without adopting any policies to reduce their emissions in the future" (Ostrom, 2010, p. 555).

## 6. Conclusion

**Synthesis for Long-term Recall:**

This article represents Elinor Ostrom's application of her Nobel Prize-winning research on commons governance to the challenge of climate change. The key takeaway is her challenge to the dominant assumption that global environmental problems require exclusively global solutions.

**The Core Problem Reframed:** Climate change is typically framed as the ultimate collective action problem--global in scope, requiring universal participation, and seemingly impossible to solve without a binding international treaty with enforcement mechanisms. Ostrom argues this framing is both theoretically flawed and practically counterproductive.

**Why Conventional Theory Falls Short:** Traditional collective action theory predicts that without external enforcement, rational actors will free ride and cooperation will fail. However, decades of empirical research on commons governance shows this prediction often fails. Small to medium-scale groups frequently solve collective action problems through trust-building, reputation, reciprocity, and face-to-face communication. The theory's weakness is treating all collective action problems as identical regardless of scale, context, or institutional arrangements.

**The Polycentric Alternative:** Rather than a single global authority, polycentric systems feature multiple, overlapping decision-making centers at different scales (local, regional, national, international) that interact competitively and cooperatively. Key advantages include:
- **Experimentation:** Different jurisdictions try different approaches, generating knowledge about what works
- **Learning:** Actors can observe and adapt successful strategies from elsewhere
- **Trust-building:** Smaller units facilitate the face-to-face interaction needed for cooperation
- **Multiple benefits:** Actions to reduce emissions often provide local co-benefits (reduced energy costs, better air quality, job creation)
- **Matching scale to problem:** Different aspects of climate change may be better addressed at different scales

**Evidence of Polycentric Climate Action:** By 2010, extensive multi-scale climate action was already occurring: over 1,000 U.S. mayors committed to emissions reductions, California passed comprehensive climate legislation, the EU Emissions Trading Scheme achieved measurable reductions, regional initiatives like RGGI emerged, and countless local efforts in building efficiency, transportation, and energy production were underway.

**Honest Assessment of Limitations:** Ostrom does not claim polycentric systems are perfect. She explicitly addresses problems including leakage (emissions shifting elsewhere), policy inconsistency, certification failures, gaming of carbon markets, and free riding. However, she argues these problems also plague global approaches and that polycentric systems have better mechanisms for detecting and correcting such failures through mutual monitoring and adaptation.

**The Practical Implication:** Stop waiting for a comprehensive global treaty that may never materialize or may be unenforceable. Instead, encourage and support climate action at all levels while continuing to work toward international coordination. Local and regional actions are not merely stopgaps--they are essential components of an effective response that builds capacity, generates learning, and creates the trust needed for larger-scale cooperation.

**For Future Recall:** When someone argues that only a global solution can address climate change, remember Ostrom's counter: the same logic was applied to metropolitan governance, and empirical research proved it wrong. Multiple overlapping authorities can coordinate effectively, innovate rapidly, and achieve outcomes that monolithic approaches cannot.

## 8. Discussion Questions

1. **Scaling Trust:** Ostrom emphasizes that trust and reciprocity are essential for successful collective action and that these are easier to build in smaller, face-to-face settings. How might the trust-building mechanisms that work at local scales be adapted or supplemented to facilitate cooperation at national and international scales? What role might technology, communication networks, or institutional design play in "scaling up" trust?

2. **Addressing Free Riding Without Global Enforcement:** Ostrom acknowledges that free riding is a legitimate concern in polycentric systems, particularly as developing nations increase emissions without adopting reduction policies. If there is no global authority to impose sanctions, what mechanisms within a polycentric framework could address free riding? Are moral suasion, reputation effects, or trade-related incentives sufficient?

3. **Balancing Experimentation and Coordination:** The polycentric approach values experimentation and diversity of approaches, but the problem of inconsistent policies creating barriers to technology adoption suggests some coordination is necessary. How do we determine the appropriate balance between local policy autonomy and harmonized standards? Who should make these determinations?

4. **Evaluating Progress Since 2010:** This article was published in 2010. In the intervening years, we have seen the Paris Agreement (a more polycentric approach than Kyoto), continued growth of subnational climate action, but also increasing global emissions. Does the evidence since 2010 support or challenge Ostrom's argument? What would she likely say about current climate governance arrangements?

## 9. Bias Check

**Reflection on Neutrality:**

Ostrom's article, while scholarly and well-evidenced, reflects certain perspectives and potential biases worth noting:

1. **Disciplinary Perspective:** As a political economist who built her career demonstrating that local governance can succeed, Ostrom may have an intellectual investment in finding that polycentric approaches are superior. Her framing tends to emphasize success stories of local governance while treating global approaches as theoretically flawed.

2. **Evidence Selection:** The empirical examples are predominantly drawn from developed countries (U.S., Europe) where institutional capacity for effective local governance exists. The applicability to developing countries with weaker governance institutions is less thoroughly examined.

3. **Treatment of Counterarguments:** While Ostrom addresses potential problems with polycentric approaches (leakage, inconsistency, gaming), the treatment is relatively brief compared to her critique of monocentric approaches. She does not deeply engage with arguments for why global coordination might be uniquely necessary for climate change (e.g., the atmosphere as a truly global commons).

4. **Timing Context:** Published in 2010, in the aftermath of the failed Copenhagen climate negotiations, the article may reflect frustration with global approaches at a particular historical moment.

5. **Normative Commitments:** Ostrom clearly values subsidiarity, local autonomy, and bottom-up governance. These are defensible values, but readers should recognize that alternative normative frameworks (emphasizing equity, global justice, or precautionary principles) might lead to different conclusions.

**Accuracy Score: 8/10**

The article presents well-documented empirical evidence and sound theoretical reasoning. The core claims about the success of polycentric governance in other domains and the existence of substantial subnational climate action are accurate. The theoretical framework is rigorous and based on decades of research. Points deducted for: (1) limited engagement with the strongest arguments for global approaches; (2) examples skewed toward developed country contexts; (3) the impossibility of definitively resolving whether polycentric or monocentric approaches would be more effective for climate change, making some claims necessarily speculative.
