# Study Partner Summary: Emergence as a Feature of Practice-based Design Research

## 1. Overview of the Document

This paper was authored by William W. Gaver, Peter Gall Krogh, Andy Boucher, and David Chatting, representing a distinguished collaboration between leading design research institutions. William Gaver and Andy Boucher are affiliated with the Interaction Research Studio at Northumbria University in London, United Kingdom, while Peter Gall Krogh works in Digital Design at Aarhus University in Denmark, and David Chatting is based in the Department of Design at Goldsmiths, University of London. Gaver is particularly notable in the HCI community as a pioneer of cultural probes methodology and research through design approaches, having shaped foundational debates about what constitutes legitimate design research within human-computer interaction. Krogh has contributed significantly to understanding how design experiments evolve over time through his work on "ways of drifting" in design research trajectories.

The document is a peer-reviewed conference paper published in the proceedings of the Designing Interactive Systems Conference (DIS '22), held virtually in June 2022 in association with an Australian host location. DIS is a premier ACM venue for design-oriented research in interactive systems, making this an appropriate forum for methodological reflection on practice-based design research. The paper is classified under CCS concepts relating to Human-centered computing and Interaction design.

The significance of this paper lies in its direct confrontation of what the authors describe as an "open secret" in the design research community: that research projects rarely unfold according to their original plans. While emergence has been extensively discussed in other disciplines such as sociology and feminist technoscience, the HCI design community has rarely reflected on emergence in representations of practice-based work. This creates a problematic tension between how research is actually conducted and how it is reported. The paper argues that this misrepresentation not only distorts understanding of design research but potentially constrains designers' ability to fully utilize their skills. By making emergence an explicit topic of scholarly discussion, the authors seek to legitimize adaptive, responsive approaches to research that honor the inherent unpredictability of design practice.

## 2. Research Overview

The central research question driving this essay concerns how practice-based design researchers should navigate, report, and evaluate the emergent qualities that characterize their work. The authors seek to understand why emergence, despite being integral to design practice, remains underrepresented in design research publications, and what strategies might help the community more openly embrace emergence as a legitimate feature of rigorous inquiry.

The paper employs a reflective essay methodology rather than empirical investigation, drawing on the authors' extensive collective experience as practice-based design researchers combined with a synthetic review of relevant literature spanning design theory, philosophy of science, sociology, and science and technology studies. This approach allows the authors to examine emergence from multiple disciplinary perspectives while grounding their analysis in the lived realities of conducting design research.

The key concept of emergence is defined in opposition to what the authors term "intention-bound" research. Borrowing from Lucy Suchman's foundational work on situated action, they characterize emergence-friendly research as "something arising out of ongoing activity, enacted rather than predetermined" (Gaver et al., 2022, p. 518). This definition positions emergence not as a failure of planning but as a productive responsiveness to external influences, material potentials, new learning, ideas, and inspirations. The authors contrast this with the "preregistration revolution" in quantitative sciences, where researchers commit to hypotheses, methods, and analyses before data collection to prevent post-hoc rationalization.

A particularly illuminating concept is the distinction between "day science" and "night science," borrowed from Nobel laureate Françoise Jacob. Day science "employs reasoning that snaps together like gears, and achieves results with the force of certainty," while night science "is a sort of workshop of the possible where the future building materials of science are made" (Gaver et al., 2022, p. 518). The authors argue that design research may be distinctive in that its most valuable contributions often emerge from the exploratory, intuitive processes Jacob associates with night science, rather than the systematic hypothesis testing of day science.

## 3. Theories of Knowledge

The paper draws on several theoretical frameworks to situate emergence within broader epistemological debates. Most prominently, the authors engage with Lucy Suchman's theory of situated action, which views human activity as emerging through embodied interactions between actors and their environments rather than as the execution of pre-formed mental plans. Suchman argues that plans "presuppose rather than specify the possibilities for actions" and "serve to orient actors rather than guide them" (Gaver et al., 2022, p. 518). This perspective fundamentally challenges cognitivist models of action that treat behavior as the implementation of mental representations.

The ethnomethodological tradition, developed by Harold Garfinkel, provides another theoretical anchor. Ethnomethodology views social order as produced and maintained through members' ongoing activities rather than as manifestations of underlying social laws. This leads to an approach characterized by "ethnomethodological indifference" toward both the adequacy of social orders being studied and the theories and methods traditionally brought to bear on them. In its pure form, this means researchers approach projects without preconceived questions or concepts, allowing understanding to emerge through encounters with settings and people.

The authors also engage with Donald Schon's concept of the "reflective practitioner" and his characterization of design as a "conversation with materials." Schon described how designers' ontologies and designs co-evolve: the words designers use to describe situations both impel and constrain their design decisions. Each "design move" reveals new opportunities and challenges, leading to subsequent moves that could not have been predicted from the project's starting point. This processual view aligns with Rittel and Webber's notion of "wicked problems," which resist systematic solution approaches because every problem can be considered a symptom of another problem.

Finally, the paper draws on performativity theory from science and technology studies, particularly the work of Marres and colleagues on "inventing the social." This perspective recognizes that research does not merely describe social realities but actively participates in their construction. If research is inherently interventionist, the authors suggest, then researchers should embrace this performative dimension rather than pretending to objectivity.

## 4. Central Arguments

The paper's central argument is that practice-based design research should openly embrace emergence as a fundamental characteristic of its methodology rather than suppressing or hiding it to conform to intention-bound research traditions. The authors contend that the mismatch between how design research is actually conducted and how it is reported creates a problematic tension that "at best leads to misrepresentations of practice-based design research, and at worst to a constraint on designers' ability to use their skills" (Gaver et al., 2022, p. 517).

The argument unfolds through several interconnected sub-claims. First, the authors establish that emergence is inherent to design practice itself. Drawing on Schon's work, they show how design involves continuous reframing of challenges, with each design move revealing new possibilities that could not have been anticipated. Krogh and colleagues' taxonomy of "ways of drifting" in design research further demonstrates that emergence operates along a spectrum, from relatively linear "accumulative" approaches to more opportunistic "probing" strategies. Regardless of where on this spectrum a project falls, "virtually all admit to at least a degree of emergence in actual practice" (Gaver et al., 2022, p. 519).

Second, the authors argue that many design methods actively encourage emergence. Cultural probes, design workbooks, technology probes, and design fictions all share the characteristic that they help designers and participants imagine new social worlds, designs, and implications simultaneously. These methods do not merely extract information from participants or help designers think about technical possibilities; they facilitate the co-emergence of design possibilities and conceptual understanding.

Third, the economic context of academic design research facilitates emergence because researchers are typically accountable to funders, participants, and reviewers for producing innovative designs and new understandings rather than for adhering strictly to their original proposals. Unlike commercial design, where clients sanction briefs that constrain emergence, academic design researchers often write their own briefs in the form of proposals and are not held accountable for responding precisely to them.

However, the authors also identify forces that inhibit emergence in practice-based design research. These include assumptions that design must "add" systematic research processes to qualify as research, pressure from technical disciplinary neighbors who present their work in intention-bound terms, and conventional paper structures that favor linear narratives from research questions to outcomes. The result is that "many practice-based design research reports adopt a relatively linear structure" that may not reflect the actual emergent character of the work (Gaver et al., 2022, p. 520).

The authors address a potential counterargument: that emergent research lacks rigor. They argue instead that "there is also a kind of rigour in attending to clues, seeking radically new perspectives and following untraveled paths, a rigour that is one of the hallmarks of good design" (Gaver et al., 2022, p. 524-525). This reconceptualization positions the skills required to navigate emergence, such as responsiveness, judgment, and the ability to recognize productive anomalies, as themselves constituting research rigor.

## 5. Evidence

The authors support their arguments through multiple forms of evidence, though as a reflective essay the paper does not present original empirical data. Instead, it synthesizes published accounts of design research, theoretical frameworks from multiple disciplines, and the authors' own extensive experience as practitioners.

From the design research literature, the authors cite concrete examples of projects that shifted focus during their execution. Gaver et al. (2010) describes a project originally conceived to support intergenerational contact that was "transformed into a study of how technology could support spirituality by a fortuitous encounter with a group of cloistered nuns" (Gaver et al., 2022, p. 520). Odom and colleagues' program on "everyday design" evolved across multiple publications, with designs changing radically alongside emerging understandings, ultimately addressing questions about the nature of design outputs that had not been articulated when the original designs were created. These examples demonstrate that emergence is not merely a theoretical possibility but a documented feature of actual design research practice.

The paper draws extensively on Krogh, Markussen, and Bang's (2015) study of ten PhD theses examining how design experiments evolved over time. This research identified five tactics or "ways of drifting" for bringing about knowledge: accumulative explorations that build knowledge step by step, serial experiments that evolve as results point in new directions, and probing approaches that allow new ideas to emerge rapidly. This empirical taxonomy provides concrete evidence that emergence operates along a spectrum in design research and that different practitioners and epistemic traditions emphasize different degrees of openness to emergent directions.

From the philosophy and sociology of science, the authors draw on Françoise Jacob's autobiographical account of "day and night science" to demonstrate that emergence is integral even to fields typically portrayed as thoroughly intention-bound. The discovery of graphene during "Saturday Night Experiments" sessions conducted by Geim and Novoselov, and the Curies' unexpected discovery of radiation's lethal effects while investigating physical properties of uranium, provide additional evidence that emergence characterizes scientific research broadly.

The paper's methodological limitations stem from its essay format and the authors' positionality. As highly successful design researchers themselves, their perspective inevitably reflects particular institutional contexts, career trajectories, and design traditions. The paper acknowledges that not all design researchers may share their commitment to emergence; some genuinely advocate for more intention-bound approaches. Additionally, the examples provided come predominantly from Anglophone design research communities and may not represent global practice-based design research traditions.

A potential weakness is that the paper does not systematically analyze a corpus of design research publications to establish how commonly emergence is suppressed in reporting versus actually absent from practice. The authors assert that many papers adopt linear structures that may misrepresent emergent processes, but this claim rests on their experience as practitioners and reviewers rather than systematic content analysis. Similarly, the twelve strategies offered in the paper's final section emerge from the authors' personal experience rather than empirical validation of their effectiveness.

The theoretical synthesis is persuasive but selective. The authors draw primarily on intellectual traditions sympathetic to emergence, particularly ethnomethodology, situated action theory, and feminist technoscience. Perspectives that might challenge the value of emergence or argue for stronger intentionality in research, such as certain traditions in design science or engineering research, receive less charitable treatment. The paper positions Design Science in the Herbert Simon tradition as essentially opposed to emergence, but proponents of that tradition might argue for more nuanced readings of Simon's work.

## 6. Conclusion

This paper makes a compelling case that practice-based design research should embrace rather than suppress emergence as a characteristic feature of its methodology. The argument synthesizes design theory, philosophy of science, sociology, and the authors' extensive practical experience to demonstrate that emergence is inherent to design practice, encouraged by many design methods, and facilitated by the economic contexts in which academic design research typically occurs. At the same time, forces including disciplinary norms, accountability structures, and reporting conventions often inhibit emergence or encourage researchers to present their work in misleadingly intention-bound terms.

For scholars in sustainability, resilience, and computing within limits, this paper offers valuable methodological resources. Research addressing wicked problems like sustainability inherently involves the kind of uncertainty and emergent learning that the authors describe. The paper legitimizes adaptive research approaches where methods, questions, and even topics evolve in response to engagement with communities and environments. This is particularly relevant for research that takes seriously the situated, contingent, and political dimensions of sustainability challenges.

The twelve strategies for encouraging, managing, narrating, and assessing emergence provide practical guidance that extends beyond design research. The suggestion to "consider anomalies to be inspirations" rather than outliers to be excluded aligns with calls for more generative approaches to sustainability research. The strategy of "seeking idiosyncratic examples of design settings" resonates with arguments for centering marginalized perspectives and alternative ways of living in sustainability scholarship. The call to "present design research as a journey, not a quest" offers narrative possibilities for research that does not know in advance what it will find.

The paper's most enduring contribution may be its reframing of rigor. Rather than locating rigor solely in adherence to predetermined plans, the authors argue for "a kind of rigour in attending to clues, seeking radically new perspectives and following untraveled paths." This suggests that the skills required to navigate emergence, including responsiveness, judgment, and recognition of productive anomalies, themselves constitute research competence. For a field like computing within limits that must grapple with unprecedented challenges, this reconceptualization of rigor may prove essential.

Critical readers should note that the paper does not resolve all tensions it identifies. The authors acknowledge that "unchecked emergence can produce endless digression, just as rigid adherence to intentions can miss opportunities." The balance between intentionality and openness remains a matter of judgment that this paper illuminates but cannot specify in advance. Additionally, the strategies offered emerge from particular institutional and cultural contexts that may not translate directly to other settings. Nonetheless, the paper's fundamental insight, that honoring emergence is compatible with rigorous research, deserves serious consideration from all practice-based researchers.

## 8. Discussion Questions

1. **Navigating emergence in funded research:** The authors argue that academic design researchers are typically not held accountable for adhering closely to their original proposals, which facilitates emergence. How might researchers navigate situations where funders, institutional review boards, or other stakeholders expect more predictable research trajectories? What strategies might help reconcile the value of emergence with accountability requirements, particularly in sustainability research where community partnerships and ethical commitments may constrain adaptive approaches?

2. **Emergence and disciplinary legitimacy:** The paper identifies tension between design research's emergence-friendly tendencies and the intention-bound norms of its "technical neighbors" in engineering and computer science. For researchers working at the intersection of design, computing, and sustainability, how might this tension manifest? Does embracing emergence risk marginalizing design-oriented sustainability research within computing disciplines, and if so, is that a price worth paying?

3. **Emergence as privilege:** The authors' examples of productive emergence often involve well-resourced research groups with established reputations that can afford to follow unexpected directions. To what extent might the ability to embrace emergence be unevenly distributed across career stages, institutional contexts, and global regions? How might early-career researchers or those in precarious positions navigate emergence when deviation from plans carries higher stakes?

4. **Limits of emergence in sustainability contexts:** The paper celebrates emergence as enabling researchers to discover insights and directions they could not have predicted. But in sustainability research contexts where urgent problems demand action, what are the limits of open-ended exploration? When should researchers commit to particular directions even at the cost of foreclosing emergent possibilities, and how might they make such judgments?
