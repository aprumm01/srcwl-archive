# Study-Partner Summary: Systemic Sustainable HCI

## 1. Overview of the Document

This paper was authored by Laetitia Bornes, Catherine Letondal, and Rob Vingerhoeds, all affiliated with the Fédération ENAC ISAE-SUPAERO ONERA at the Université de Toulouse in France. The research team brings together expertise spanning human-computer interaction, systems engineering, and design methodology. Bornes appears to be the lead researcher, with prior work on systemic design methods supporting sustainable design of interactive systems. Letondal contributes expertise in HCI design methodology, while Vingerhoeds offers background in systems engineering and modeling approaches. Their institutional home positions them at the intersection of aerospace engineering and human factors research, which informs their systems-oriented perspective on design.

The document is a peer-reviewed conference paper published in the proceedings of DIS '24 (Designing Interactive Systems Conference), held July 1-5, 2024, at the IT University of Copenhagen, Denmark. The 17-page paper follows ACM publication standards and represents original empirical research combining methodological development with workshop-based evaluation. As a DIS publication, it targets the premier venue for research connecting design practice with interactive systems scholarship.

The paper's significance lies in its attempt to bridge a recognized gap in Sustainable Human-Computer Interaction (SHCI) research. While the SHCI community has increasingly called for systemic approaches to address sustainability challenges, concrete methodologies and tools for designers have remained scarce. This work responds directly to that call by developing and empirically testing a collaborative modeling methodology that equips designers to understand, anticipate, and mitigate rebound effects. The paper introduces the concept of "Systemic Sustainable HCI" as a new research direction, positioning systemic modeling not merely as an analytical technique but as an integral component of the design process itself. This represents a meaningful contribution to the field's evolution from persuasive and behavior-change interventions toward more structurally-oriented approaches to sustainability.

## 2. Research Overview

The central research question driving this work asks whether collaborative systemic modeling can help HCI designers understand and mitigate rebound effects when designing digital products and services. More specifically, the authors investigate how such modeling approaches might be integrated into existing design processes and what value professional designers perceive in these methods. The research addresses a practical gap: while systems thinking and system dynamics have been proposed as relevant frameworks for understanding rebound effects, concrete examples of their application in HCI design contexts have been lacking.

The authors employed a methodological prototype approach, developing a collaborative modeling methodology that combines influence diagrams with dynamic modeling and simulation. They then conducted two half-day workshops with a total of ten professional designers recruited through the French-speaking UX professional network Flupa. Participants worked on a case study involving Vinted, a second-hand clothing resale platform, which provided a real-world context with documented rebound effects. The workshops utilized Miro boards for collaborative diagramming and a custom interactive modeling tool called Magnitude for simulation. Data collection included observation of workshop activities, semi-structured interviews with four participants, and analysis of participant-generated artifacts such as influence diagrams and design ideas.

The key concepts undergirding this research include rebound effects, which the authors define carefully. They explain that "rebound effects typically refer to those that have negative impacts (such as increased energy, material consumption, emissions and/or pollutions), often by causing an increase in demand and production" (Bornes et al., 2024, p. 3047). The paper distinguishes between direct rebound effects (when efficiency gains lead to increased use of the same product), indirect rebound effects (when savings enable consumption of other energy-intensive goods), and systemic or structural effects (when behavioral changes become visible at the sociotechnical or socioeconomic scale). The concept of "backfire" describes situations where negative consequences exceed initial savings entirely.

## 3. Theories of Knowledge

The paper draws on several interconnected theoretical frameworks that inform both its methodology and its analytical perspective. The primary theoretical foundation is systems thinking, which the authors describe as "a holistic and interdisciplinary approach that provides a framework for understanding complex problems within the context of larger systems instead of isolating individual parts and emphasizes understanding how components within a system interact" (Bornes et al., 2024, p. 3047). Systems thinking originated in the 1960s and provides the epistemological basis for viewing digital products not as isolated artifacts but as components within larger sociotechnical systems whose effects ripple outward in complex, often counterintuitive ways.

System dynamics, a mathematical modeling methodology developed by Jay Forrester, serves as the operational framework for the quantitative aspects of the methodology. System dynamics enables researchers and practitioners to understand, analyze, and simulate the behavior of complex systems over time, finding applications across business management, public policy, and environmental science.

The authors also draw heavily on systemic design, which they characterize as "a new interdisciplinary field of research at the crossroads of systems thinking and design thinking, which develops methods and tools to tackle complex, multi-scale problems at the social and sociotechnical level" (Bornes et al., 2024, p. 3048). The Systemic Design Toolkit provides a seven-stage methodology that the authors adapt for their purposes. Additionally, group model building serves as the participatory methodology enabling stakeholders to collaboratively construct conceptual models of complex situations.

Donella Meadows' leverage points framework provides the theoretical structure for categorizing intervention strategies, ranging from acting on constants and parameters (easiest to implement) to changing the goals or underlying paradigms of systems (most difficult but most effective in the long term).

## 4. Central Arguments

The paper's central argument holds that collaborative systemic modeling represents a promising and practical approach for integrating sustainability considerations into HCI design processes, specifically by helping designers identify, understand, and develop strategies to mitigate rebound effects. The authors contend that existing SHCI approaches have remained too theoretical, provocative, or exploratory to provide designers with actionable methods. As they state, the SHCI community has "moved towards more diverse, qualitative and speculative SHCI research" (Bornes et al., 2024, p. 3046), but this movement has not translated into concrete tools for practitioners addressing urgent sustainability challenges.

A key sub-claim establishes the HCI community's unique positioning to address rebound effects. The authors argue that "given its pivotal position between humans and technology, its ability to study sociotechnical phenomena, and its holistic and interdisciplinary approach, the HCI community could play a pioneering role in considering indirect and rebound effects" (Bornes et al., 2024, p. 3046). This positions the contribution not as peripheral to HCI concerns but as central to the field's distinctive competencies.

The paper further argues that influence diagrams and causal loop diagrams are well-suited for designers because they align with existing design practices around visualization and collaborative sensemaking. The workshop findings demonstrated that "most participants spontaneously expressed their understanding using heuristic and causal diagrams," which "allows us to posit that causal loop diagrams are well-suited for qualitatively representing systemic situations that involve direct and indirect effects" (Bornes et al., 2024, p. 3055). This suggests lower barriers to adoption than purely mathematical approaches.

The authors also advance a methodological argument about the complementary value of qualitative and quantitative modeling. They position dynamic modeling not as a predictive tool but as a "prospective tool, enabling reflection through the manipulation and exploration of potential futures" (Bornes et al., 2024, p. 3049). Nine of ten participants affirmed the relevance of the quantitative aspect for simulation purposes, with one designer noting that quantification "allows us to concretely visualize the impact of actions and compare situations" (Bornes et al., 2024, p. 3057).

## 5. Evidence

The primary evidence supporting the paper's claims derives from the two workshops conducted with ten professional designers. The participant sample included individuals with varying relationships to sustainability (some with professional focus on sustainability, others without) and varying familiarity with systemic design (ranging from no prior knowledge to having read about and seen causal loop diagrams). This diversity enabled the researchers to assess how the methodology might work across the spectrum of practitioners likely to encounter it in real-world contexts.

The workshops generated multiple forms of evidence. First, the nine schematic representations produced by participants during pre-workshop preparation demonstrated spontaneous use of diagrammatic thinking, with eight of the nine representations incorporating diagrams that employed causal links and connections. Second, the collaborative influence diagrams produced during the workshops (documented in Figures 5 and 6) showed participants successfully identifying variables across categories including platform strategy, users, products, purchases, transportation, and environmental impact. Third, the design ideas generated during and after the workshops (catalogued in Table 7) revealed that six of ten designers generated new ideas following the modeling exercise, though only three of the 23 total ideas were completely original to the workshop process.

The semi-structured interviews provided qualitative evidence of participant perceptions. Designers expressed that preliminary investigation "helps building a first knowledge about the existing direct and indirect effects, with a systemic lens, and allows a better understanding of the subject, its magnitude, its implications" (Bornes et al., 2024, p. 3058). Regarding quantitative modeling, one participant explained that it "allows us to concretely visualize the impact of actions and compare situations. It reminds me of projects with clients we work on, especially those related to environmental considerations, where indeed, they want to quantify and measure, that's the transition to action" (Bornes et al., 2024, p. 3057-3058).

The paper acknowledges several methodological limitations that qualify the evidence. The participant sample consisted entirely of French speakers who volunteered, potentially introducing recruitment bias. The workshops did not include the diversity of stakeholders (environmental economists, policymakers, engineers) that the full methodology recommends. The case study relied on literature data rather than primary data collection, meaning the quantitative consolidation of the influence diagram was not empirically tested with participants. The short workshop timeframe (half-day sessions) could not capture the extended timeline over which real implementation would unfold. Finally, the study employed only influence diagrams and stock-and-flow modeling, excluding other potentially relevant approaches such as agent-based modeling. These limitations suggest the findings represent preliminary evidence of feasibility and promise rather than definitive validation of effectiveness.

## 6. Conclusion

This paper makes a significant contribution to Sustainable HCI by moving beyond theoretical calls for systemic approaches and toward concrete, empirically-tested methodology. The authors propose "Systemic Sustainable HCI" as a new research area aimed at building systemic concepts and tools to support sustainable HCI efforts. Their collaborative modeling methodology combines qualitative influence diagramming with quantitative system dynamics simulation, offering designers a structured process for identifying rebound effects, understanding their magnitudes, comparing mitigation strategies, and monitoring outcomes over time.

For recall six months from now, remember this paper as the "Vinted rebound effects workshop paper" that tested whether professional designers could use systems thinking tools in their practice. The key finding was encouraging: designers found the methodology relevant and useful, particularly for structuring their thinking about complex sustainability problems and for comparing design alternatives through simulation. Influence diagrams helped structure ideas and identify gaps, while dynamic modeling enabled strategy comparison. However, the quantitative aspects presented challenges, with four of ten participants expressing difficulty with consolidating the influence diagram into quantitative form.

The case study of Vinted illuminates how rebound effects manifest in digital platforms. The second-hand clothing platform, while ostensibly supporting sustainability, generates rebound effects including: increased impulsive purchases (25% of transactions), increased travel distances for shipping (after removal of local search filters), transition of users toward "consu-merchant" identities that change their relationship with clothing consumption, and decreased donations to charitable organizations. These examples make tangible the abstract concept of rebound effects and illustrate why simple assumptions about "sustainable" digital services can mislead.

The framework proposed in Figure 9 integrates the modeling methodology across five design stages: Discovery, Interpretation, Ideation, Experimentation, and Evolution. This positions systemic modeling not as a one-time analysis but as an iterative component woven throughout the design process. The seven future research directions identified (narrative foundation, scenario exploration, deeper comprehension of digital rebound effects, best practices for quantitative modeling, tailored modeling tools, policy maker involvement, and working within planetary boundaries) chart a research agenda that could occupy the SHCI community for years.

For seminar discussion, this paper exemplifies the tension in SHCI between accessible methods that designers will actually adopt and rigorous methods that produce valid insights. The authors navigate this by proposing influence diagrams as an entry point that aligns with designers' existing visual thinking practices, while acknowledging that quantitative modeling may require additional training and support.

## 8. Discussion Questions

**Question 1: Scalability and Stakeholder Inclusion.** The authors acknowledge that their workshops included only designers, while the full methodology recommends involving decision-makers, environmental specialists, and engineers. How might the dynamics and outcomes of the collaborative modeling process change when these diverse stakeholders with potentially conflicting interests and knowledge bases participate? What power dynamics might emerge, and how could facilitators navigate them?

**Question 2: The Greenwashing Risk.** Designer D5 raised concerns that the quantitative methodology could be misused for "greenwashing" by constructing unrealistic models that make design decisions appear more sustainable than they are. Given that the modeling depends heavily on assumptions and estimated values, what safeguards or validation practices might prevent such misuse? Is there an inherent tension between making the methodology accessible to designers and maintaining scientific rigor?

**Question 3: Rebound Effects of the Methodology Itself.** The paper focuses on using systemic modeling to address rebound effects of digital products. However, the methodology itself requires resources (designer time, workshop facilitation, modeling tools, organizational buy-in). Could there be "meta-rebound effects" where companies adopt sustainability methodologies as a way to justify continued growth and production rather than questioning whether certain products should exist at all? How does this methodology relate to more radical SHCI positions like post-growth computing?

**Question 4: Temporal and Spatial Boundaries.** The Vinted case study reveals rebound effects that manifest across different temporal scales (immediate purchase behavior vs. long-term cultural shifts in clothing consumption) and spatial scales (individual transactions vs. charitable organization impacts). How should designers determine the appropriate boundaries for their systemic models, and what gets lost when boundaries must be drawn? Does the methodology risk privileging effects that are easier to model over those that are more diffuse but potentially more significant?

## 9. Bias Check

**Neutrality Reflection:** The paper maintains a generally balanced scholarly tone while clearly advocating for its proposed methodology. The authors appropriately acknowledge limitations of their workshop study, including recruitment bias, limited stakeholder diversity, reliance on literature rather than primary data for the case study, and the artificial time compression of the workshop format. They present both enthusiastic participant responses and skeptical ones, including Designer D5's concerns about greenwashing potential. The discussion section thoughtfully addresses methodological scope limitations, noting that "the fact that this methodology integrates well into a design process and is perceived as useful by designers for understanding rebound effects and comparing design strategies does not guarantee that rebound effects will be effectively reduced, let alone to what extent" (Bornes et al., 2024, p. 3059).

However, the paper exhibits some optimism bias in its framing. The positive findings (9 of 10 participants found the quantitative aspect relevant) receive prominent treatment, while challenges (4 of 10 expressed difficulty with quantitative consolidation) appear later. The selection of Vinted as a case study with well-documented rebound effects may have made the methodology appear more successful than it would with less-studied systems. The authors' own development of the Magnitude modeling tool creates potential conflicts of interest in evaluating the methodology's effectiveness.

**Accuracy Score: 8/10.** The paper demonstrates strong methodological transparency and appropriate qualification of claims. The research design is clearly described, limitations are acknowledged, and the theoretical grounding is well-established. The score is reduced from perfect due to: (1) the preliminary nature of the evidence (two workshops, ten participants, no longitudinal follow-up), (2) potential optimism bias in presenting findings, and (3) the untested assumption that workshop insights would translate to real-world design practice with actual organizational constraints.
