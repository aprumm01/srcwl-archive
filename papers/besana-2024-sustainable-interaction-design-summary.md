## 1. Overview of the Document

**Authors and Affiliations:**

This systematic literature review was authored by four researchers with expertise spanning design, digital media, and computer science:

- **Nicola Besana** (Department of Design, Politecnico di Milano, Italy) - corresponding author with expertise in design research
- **Peter Gall Krogh** (Department of Digital Design and Information Studies, Aarhus University, Denmark) - specializing in digital design
- **Marianne Graves Petersen** (Department of Computer Science, Aarhus University, Denmark) - expertise in computer science and HCI
- **Davide Spallazzo** (Department of Design, Politecnico di Milano, Italy) - design research background

The collaborative effort between Italian and Danish institutions represents a significant cross-institutional research endeavor, bringing together perspectives from design practice and computer science traditions. This interdisciplinary composition is particularly relevant given that Sustainable Interaction Design (SID) sits at the intersection of Human-Computer Interaction (HCI), Interaction Design (IxD), and sustainability studies.

**Document Type:**

This is a peer-reviewed systematic literature review published in *Sustainability* (MDPI), an open-access journal. The article was received on June 14, 2024, revised on August 20, 2024, accepted on August 22, 2024, and published on August 29, 2024. The research followed the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) model, lending methodological rigor to the review process.

**Field and Significance:**

The paper occupies a critical position within the fields of Human-Computer Interaction (HCI), Interaction Design, and sustainability research. Since Eli Blevis introduced the concept of Sustainable Interaction Design in 2007, the field has expanded significantly, yet no comprehensive systematic review had mapped how the discourse has evolved across methodology and impact dimensions. This study fills that gap by analyzing 185 publications from 2000-2023, making it one of the most comprehensive reviews of SID literature to date. The research is particularly significant because it identifies an emergent shift in how sustainability is conceptualized within design discourse and proposes an expansion of Blevis's original framework from two dimensions (sustainability "in design" and "through design") to three dimensions (adding "by design").

## 2. Research Overview

**Central Research Question:**

The primary objective of this study is to systematically analyze and map the conceptualization and application of "Sustainable Interaction Design" within scholarly discourse. The authors seek to clarify the nuanced dimensions and theoretical foundations that scholars use when addressing SID, with the ultimate goal of deepening understanding of its complex relationships with both design methodology and the broader framework of sustainability.

**Who Conducted the Study:**

The research team consisted of four academics from Politecnico di Milano (Italy) and Aarhus University (Denmark). The collaborative nature reflects the transnational character of SID research itself. Funding was provided by the Aarhus University Research Foundation (AUFF) grant no. AUFF-F-2022-6-10, and the paper was developed in relation to an Erasmus+ grant (KA220-HED-2024-005) aimed at building curriculum for sustainable interaction design.

**Data and Methods:**

The study employed a systematic literature review methodology following PRISMA guidelines. The initial search used the query: *TITLE-ABS-KEY ("sustainable interaction design" OR "sustainable HCI" OR "sustainable human computer interaction" OR "SHCI" OR "SID")* across Scopus and Web of Science databases. This search yielded 469 results published between 2000 and December 2023. After removing 117 duplicates, 352 publications remained for abstract screening. Following the application of five exclusion criteria for abstract screening and three additional criteria for full-text screening, 185 studies were included in the final analysis. The remaining publications were coded using MaxQda qualitative analysis software through three analytical lenses: (A) Framing of sustainability, (B) Role of design, and (C) Recurrent and emerging topics.

**Key Concepts Defined:**

The paper distinguishes between three ways sustainability is promoted in interactive technologies:
- **Sustainability by design**: Interactive technologies incorporate sustainability into the way they are designed, constituting an integral aspect of the artifact or system's design
- **Sustainability through design**: Designed interactive technologies function as instruments to propagate sustainable behaviors
- **Sustainability in designing**: How interactive technologies are conceived, encompassing design processes and methodologies where sustainability emanates from the design processes themselves

**Direct Verbatim Quote:**

"In 2007, Eli Blevis introduced the concept of Sustainable Interaction Design (SID) within the field of Human-Computer Interaction (HCI), aiming to prioritize sustainability in Interaction Design (IxD). Since then, the body of literature on SID has expanded significantly, revealing its complex nature" (Besana et al., 2024, p. 1).

## 3. Theories of Knowledge

**Key Theoretical Frameworks:**

The paper draws on several foundational theoretical frameworks that have shaped Sustainable Interaction Design:

1. **Blevis's Original SID Framework (2007)**: The foundational theory that defined Sustainable Interaction Design as "the act of making or informing choices about future ways of being, using sustainability as a lens to evaluate design values, methods, and artifacts" (Besana et al., 2024, p. 2). Blevis emphasized environmental sustainability and provided a rubric for assessing material effects in terms of use, reuse, and disposal.

2. **Mankoff and Blevis's Two-Strand Framework**: This framework distinguishes between "sustainability in design" and "sustainability through design" as primary approaches for integrating sustainability into Interaction Design. As the authors explain, "'Sustainability in design': This approach involves using sustainability as a critical lens during the design process of interactive technologies. It encompasses four core dimensions that guide the development of sustainable interactive concepts and artifacts" (Besana et al., 2024, p. 7-8).

3. **Brundtland Report's Tripartite Classification**: The UN's World Commission on Environment and Development framework that encompasses "environmental, social, and economic concerns" (Besana et al., 2024, p. 6). The authors note that "scholars often refer to the original tripartite classification of sustainability—encompassing environmental, social, and economic concerns—as outlined in the so-called Brundtland report" (Besana et al., 2024, p. 6).

4. **Quadruple Bottom Line Framework**: Building on DiSalvo and colleagues' work, Knowles and colleagues proposed "an alternative framework to the seminal 'triple bottom line' concept, proposing a 'quadruple bottom line' (personal, social, environmental, and economic)" (Besana et al., 2024, p. 2).

**Terminology Definitions:**

- **Sustainable Interaction Design (SID)**: "The act of making or informing choices about future ways of being, using sustainability as a lens to evaluate design values, methods, and artifacts" (Besana et al., 2024, p. 2).
- **Sustainable Human-Computer Interaction (SHCI)**: The broader field encompassing sustainability research within HCI.
- **Eco-feedback technologies**: "Mechanisms that provide feedback on individual or group behaviors, with the goal of reducing environmental impact" (Besana et al., 2024, p. 13).
- **Collapse informatics**: An approach that "involves guiding design choices by considering the global scarcity of resources, particularly related to informatics and digital artifacts, and how to plan for a future marked by resource shortages" (Besana et al., 2024, p. 14-15).

## 4. Central Arguments

**Central Argument:**

The authors argue that the field of Sustainable Interaction Design has evolved beyond Blevis's original bipartite framework and now requires a tripartite conceptualization. They propose rephrasing "sustainability in design" as "sustainability by design" to emphasize sustainability as an inherent (by-product) feature integrated into interactive technologies. The central thesis is that sustainability in SID can be promoted in three distinct ways: *by*, *through*, and *in* design.

As the authors state: "To reflect these insights, we propose rephrasing 'sustainability in design' as 'sustainability by design' to emphasize sustainability as an inherent (by-product) feature integrated into interactive technologies. This modification gives space to the following three ways in which (interaction) design promotes sustainability" (Besana et al., 2024, p. 9).

**Key Sub-Claims:**

1. **Environmental Bias in SID Research**: The authors demonstrate through their analysis that "much of the research in this field predominantly emphasizes the environmental dimension of sustainability, often at the expense of the social and economic aspects" (Besana et al., 2024, p. 17). Of 179 publications addressing environmental concerns, only 40 addressed social concerns and a mere 8 addressed economic concerns.

2. **Emergence of "Sustainability in Designing"**: The paper argues that a significant subset of studies is now focusing on the sustainability of design processes themselves—what they term "sustainability in designing." These studies "emphasize designing interactive technologies in ways that are 'indirectly sustainable'. Here, researchers go beyond assessing the sustainability of the final product, extending their analysis to the methodologies and methods employed in the design process" (Besana et al., 2024, p. 12).

3. **Need for Holistic Approaches**: The authors argue that "there is a growing recognition of the need for an inclusive approach that addresses all aspects of sustainability, including communal and social aspects" (Besana et al., 2024, p. 7). They emphasize that "addressing all three dimensions—(i) environmental (quality), (ii) social (equity), and (iii) economic (prosperity)—is essential for developing effective Sustainable Interaction Design solutions" (Besana et al., 2024, p. 7).

4. **Research Gap in Economic Sustainability**: The paper identifies a critical gap: "Economic sustainability was the least addressed concern in the papers reviewed. Except for a few publications, discussions on economic sustainability—both political and economic—were notably absent" (Besana et al., 2024, p. 11).

**Qualitative Evidence:**

The paper provides detailed analysis of specific publications that exemplify the emerging "sustainability in designing" approach. For example, they cite studies on "sustainable identity" and the creativity of the designer that discuss "incorporating Design-in-Use principles, which involve using sociotechnical and cooperative design methods to create ethically sustainable systems through participatory user involvement" (Besana et al., 2024, p. 8).

The authors also present visual evidence through Venn diagrams (Figures 2 and 3) showing the distribution of publications across sustainability dimensions and design approaches, and a beeswarm chart (Figure 4) revealing significant gaps where research clusters and where it is sparse.

**Methodological Limitations:**

The authors acknowledge several important limitations:

1. **Database Constraints**: "The scope of the initial selection of papers may be constrained by the databases used. While we primarily relied on the Scopus and Web of Science databases and made efforts to supplement these with external libraries, some relevant contributions may have been overlooked" (Besana et al., 2024, p. 16).

2. **Subjectivity in Coding**: "A limitation may be subjectivity in interpretation during the qualitative analysis, which may have influenced the screening and coding of contributions. It is possible that some publications were categorized in a way that does not perfectly align with the selected analysis criteria" (Besana et al., 2024, p. 16).

3. **Exclusion of Ethical Analysis**: "This study does not address the ethical implications present in the various contributions, which could be a focus for future research. In our analysis, beyond the sustainability debate, ethical considerations were not a chosen lens" (Besana et al., 2024, p. 17).

4. **Interpretation Challenges**: "Many publications lacked clear and explicit articulation on this subject. As a result, our approach relied heavily on interpretation, adhering as closely as possible to the predefined definitions of the three categories" (Besana et al., 2024, p. 12).

## 6. Conclusion

**Synthesis for Future Recall:**

This 2024 systematic literature review by Besana, Krogh, Petersen, and Spallazzo represents a landmark mapping of how Sustainable Interaction Design (SID) has evolved since Eli Blevis introduced the concept in 2007. The study analyzed 185 publications using the PRISMA methodology and qualitative coding through MaxQda software.

**Key Takeaways:**

1. **The field has an environmental bias**: Despite sustainability being conceptualized as encompassing environmental, social, and economic dimensions (the Brundtland framework), SID research overwhelmingly focuses on environmental concerns (179 papers) while neglecting social (40 papers) and especially economic (8 papers) dimensions. This represents "the elephant in the room for Sustainable HCI."

2. **A new third dimension has emerged**: The original Blevis/Mankoff framework distinguished between "sustainability in design" (the artifact itself being sustainable) and "sustainability through design" (the artifact promoting sustainable behaviors). This study identifies a third dimension—"sustainability in designing"—which concerns making the design process itself sustainable.

3. **Proposed framework expansion**: The authors propose renaming "sustainability in design" to "sustainability by design" to better capture the inherent quality of sustainability in artifacts, while adding "sustainability in designing" as a formal third category. This tripartite framework (by, through, in) better captures how contemporary SID research actually operates.

4. **Nine recurring/emerging themes** shape the field: eco-feedback, behavioral change, persuasive technologies, design for attachment, HCI activism and feminism, e-waste, permaculture, collapse informatics, and bio-materials.

5. **Research gaps identified**: The intersection of all three design approaches (by, through, and in design) with all three sustainability dimensions (environmental, social, economic) is severely underexplored. No studies comprehensively addressed all six dimensions simultaneously.

**Implications for Practice and Future Research:**

For interaction designers and HCI researchers, this review suggests the need to consciously integrate social equity and economic prosperity considerations alongside environmental concerns. The emergence of "sustainability in designing" as a category indicates growing interest in participatory, community-based, and ethically-grounded design methodologies that embed sustainability into how design work is conducted, not just what is designed.

Future research directions include: (1) deeper investigation of economic sustainability in interactive technology design, (2) exploring connections to Service Design which has long addressed social sustainability, (3) investigating relationships between SID and Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) frameworks, and (4) ethical implications of SID interventions.

## 8. Discussion Questions

1. **On the Environmental Bias**: The study reveals that 179 of 185 papers addressed environmental sustainability while only 8 addressed economic sustainability. What structural, historical, or disciplinary factors might explain this imbalance in SID research? How might the field's roots in computer science and HCI—which traditionally prioritize technical solutions—contribute to this gap?

2. **On the Proposed Framework Expansion**: The authors propose expanding Blevis's original bipartite framework (in/through design) to a tripartite framework (by/through/in designing). Does this expansion meaningfully change how we conceptualize sustainable design practice, or does "sustainability in designing" simply describe good participatory design methodology that should have been assumed all along?

3. **On Economic Sustainability**: The paper identifies economic sustainability as the "elephant in the room" in SID research. What would it mean to design interactive technologies that promote economic sustainability? Are there inherent tensions between economic sustainability (which might emphasize growth or efficiency) and environmental sustainability (which might emphasize degrowth or sufficiency)?

4. **On Emerging Topics**: The study identifies "collapse informatics"—designing for a future of resource scarcity—as an emerging theme. In what ways does this approach challenge the dominant paradigm of technology design that assumes continued resource availability and economic growth? What ethical questions arise when designers plan for societal collapse?

## 9. Bias Check

**Reflection on Neutrality:**

The paper demonstrates strong methodological rigor through its use of the PRISMA framework, clear inclusion/exclusion criteria, and transparent reporting of limitations. The authors acknowledge potential subjectivity in their qualitative coding and the constraints of their database selection.

However, several potential biases warrant consideration:

1. **Disciplinary Bias**: All four authors come from design and computer science backgrounds, which may influence their interpretation of what constitutes "sustainability" and their relative emphasis on design methodology over, say, policy or systems-level interventions.

2. **Institutional Bias**: The study was funded by Aarhus University and developed in relation to an Erasmus+ curriculum development grant. The authors may have incentives to frame SID in ways that justify expanded curriculum and continued research funding.

3. **Western Academic Bias**: The study relies on Scopus and Web of Science databases, which may underrepresent research from non-Western contexts or published in non-English languages. Sustainability challenges and design approaches in the Global South may be systematically underrepresented.

4. **Definitional Bias**: By focusing specifically on publications that reference "Sustainable Interaction Design" or related acronyms, the study may exclude relevant work that addresses similar concerns under different terminology (e.g., "green computing," "ICT for development," "appropriate technology").

5. **Temporal Bias**: The heavy emphasis on Blevis's 2007 framework as the foundational reference point may privilege a particular historical and disciplinary perspective on what SID should be.

**Accuracy Score: 8/10**

The paper demonstrates high methodological rigor, transparent reporting, and appropriate acknowledgment of limitations. The quantitative analysis appears sound, and the qualitative interpretations are well-supported by evidence. The score is not higher because (a) the inherent subjectivity in qualitative coding introduces some uncertainty in categorical assignments, (b) the database selection may have excluded relevant work, and (c) the paper does not critically examine whether the Blevis framework itself is the appropriate foundation for organizing the field.
