## 2. Research Overview

**Central Research Question:**
The article addresses a fundamental question: What should computing research look like if we take seriously the scientific evidence that humanity is approaching or has exceeded planetary-scale ecological limits? The authors ask: "What hidden assumptions about the future are embedded in most computing research? What possible or even probable futures are we ignoring? What work should we be doing to respond to fundamental planetary limits, and to the ecological and energy constraints that global society faces over the coming years and decades?" (Nardi et al., 2018, p. 86).

**Who Conducted the Study:**
The research was conducted by the founding members and key contributors to the LIMITS research community, which has been building since 2015. The authors represent diverse institutional backgrounds spanning multiple countries (USA, New Zealand, Sweden, UAE) and draw from computer science, information science, social science, ecology, agriculture, and earth sciences. The work was supported by NSF under Grants No. CCF-1442749 and IIS-0644415.

**Data and Methods:**
This is a conceptual review article rather than an empirical study. The authors synthesize literature from multiple fields including ecological economics (Herman Daly's steady-state economics), archaeology (Joseph Tainter's work on societal collapse), sustainable human-computer interaction (SHCI), crisis informatics, and ICTD. They also draw on findings from three LIMITS workshops held between 2015-2017 at University of California Irvine, Westmont College in Santa Barbara, and in Toronto (co-located with ICT4S). Workshop participants came from institutions in Abu Dhabi, Canada, Hong Kong, Pakistan, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, the UK, and the US, with funding from Facebook and Google.

**Key Concepts Defined:**
The article introduces "computing within limits" or "LIMITS" as a new framing for computing research that "integrates three topics: current and near-future ecological, material, and energy limits; the ways new forms of computing may help support wellbeing while living within these limits; and the impact these limits are likely to have on the field of computing" (Nardi et al., 2018, p. 87).

**Direct Verbatim Quote:**
"LIMITS sees ecological and environmental issues as a 'predicament'— that is, a situation for which there are not likely to be clear-cut 'solutions' but rather a constellation of complex issues that requires broad new assumptions and approaches" (Nardi et al., 2018, p. 87).

## 3. Theories of Knowledge

**Key Theoretical Frameworks:**

The article draws on several interconnected theoretical frameworks:

1. **Ecological Economics (Herman Daly's Steady-State Economy):** The authors build on economist Herman Daly's critique of growth-oriented economics and his proposal for a steady-state economy. Daly argues that "a single-minded focus on growing the economy comes at the eventual cost of decreasing human well-being and quality of life" (Nardi et al., 2018, p. 88). The steady-state economy concept proposes that "a steady-state economy would maintain material throughput at a rate that is largely stable across time and that remains within ecological limits" while noting that "culture and society need not be static: 'Not only is quality free to evolve, but its development is positively encouraged in certain directions'" (Nardi et al., 2018, p. 88).

2. **Collapse Theory (Joseph Tainter):** The article incorporates archaeologist Joseph Tainter's analysis from *The Collapse of Complex Societies*, which "argues that civilizations eventually collapse, declining over a period of decades or centuries" through "a process that arises from increasing societal complexity, which, over time, creates burdens for systems that they eventually cannot sustain" (Nardi et al., 2018, p. 88).

3. **Planetary Boundaries Framework (Rockstrom et al.):** The authors reference work on "planetary boundaries that must not be transgressed" (Nardi et al., 2018, p. 87) as foundational for understanding physical limits to growth.

4. **Sustainable Human-Computer Interaction (SHCI):** Drawing on Eli Blevis's "Sustainable Interaction Design" as "a primary source, offering a rubric to identify how interaction designs lead to material effects, as well as several principles for engaging in sustainable interaction design" (Nardi et al., 2018, p. 89).

**Key Terminology:**

- **Predicament:** "A situation for which there are not likely to be clear-cut 'solutions' but rather a constellation of complex issues that requires broad new assumptions and approaches" (Nardi et al., 2018, p. 87).
- **Cornucopian paradigm:** A worldview "where the design of new services stimulates demand, which drives growth of increased infrastructure capacity, which then cycles back to enable the design of new services in a self-perpetuating cycle" (Nardi et al., 2018, p. 88).
- **Jevons paradox:** The phenomenon where "more efficient technologies often encourage greater use of a resource, reducing or eliminating savings" (Nardi et al., 2018, p. 92).
- **Disintermediation:** "The process of leveraging technology to supplant 'middleman' actors in resource chains" (Nardi et al., 2018, p. 92).

## 4. Central Arguments

**Central Argument:**
The authors argue that computing research has been fundamentally misdirected by its implicit assumption of perpetual growth, and must undergo a paradigm shift to address ecological and material limits. They contend that "the root of much of computing research has been driven predominantly by growth-oriented visions of society's future" (Nardi et al., 2018, p. 86), while scientific evidence indicates this growth trajectory is unsustainable.

The authors state: "Most computing work is premised on industrial civilization's default worldview in which ongoing economic growth is both achievable and desirable. This growth-focused worldview, however, is at odds with findings from many other scientific fields, which see growth as deeply problematic for ecological and social reasons" (Nardi et al., 2018, p. 86).

**Key Sub-Claims:**

1. **Computing's assumptions need examination:** "Computing researchers and practitioners are often seen as inventing the future. As such, we are implicitly also in the business of predicting the future. We plot trajectories for the future in the problems we select, the assumptions we make about technology and societal trends, and the ways we evaluate research" (Nardi et al., 2018, p. 86).

2. **Growth paradigm is problematic:** The authors challenge the "cornucopian paradigm" noting that "the idea that exponential growth of computing capacity and an ever-expanding infrastructure for computing will continue into the future is usually taken for granted" (Nardi et al., 2018, p. 88).

3. **Limits are already being encountered:** "Humanity is rapidly approaching, or has already exceeded, a variety of planet-scale limits related to the global climate system, fossil fuels, raw materials, and biocapacity" (Nardi et al., 2018, p. 86). They note we have "already exceeded many such limits through, for example, overfishing, deforestation, soil depletion, falling water tables, rising temperatures, and emitting CO2" (Nardi et al., 2018, p. 88).

4. **Computing must contribute to transition:** "We encourage those working in computing to build systems and envision worlds that are neither reliant on nor contributing to runaway growth" (Nardi et al., 2018, p. 91).

5. **Well-being over productivity:** LIMITS proposes "a shift from emphasis on standards of living and material productivity to an emphasis on long-term wellbeing" (Nardi et al., 2018, p. 87).

## 5. Evidence

**Evidence Supporting Arguments:**

The authors marshal evidence from multiple scientific domains:

**Ecological and Environmental Evidence:**
The article cites extensive research on planetary limits, noting that we have "already exceeded many such limits through, for example, overfishing, deforestation, soil depletion, falling water tables, rising temperatures, and emitting CO2 and other greenhouse gases at rates that dangerously increase their concentrations in the atmosphere" (Nardi et al., 2018, p. 88). They reference Rockstrom et al.'s planetary boundaries framework and Steffen et al.'s work on "planetary boundaries: Guiding human development on a changing planet" (Science, 2015).

**Economic Evidence:**
The authors draw on Herman Daly's ecological economics to argue that the current growth paradigm produces negative outcomes: "Such growth results in, for example, charging for things that used to be free, the health consequences of polluting the environment, and decreasing long-term possibilities to produce food or earn a livelihood" (Nardi et al., 2018, p. 88). They also note that "despite such unprecedented prosperity, global income inequality is increasing. Wealth is accumulating in the hands of fewer and fewer astoundingly rich persons. Poverty is widespread" (Nardi et al., 2018, p. 90).

**Archaeological Evidence:**
Joseph Tainter's analysis of collapsed civilizations (Maya, Mesopotamians, Minoans, Inca, Romans, Egyptians) provides historical evidence that societal collapse is a recurring pattern related to complexity and resource limits.

**Case Studies from Computing Research:**
The authors cite specific LIMITS-relevant computing research:
- Patterson's 2015 study of the 2010 earthquake in Haiti found that "the regrowth of infrastructures was occurring in a more distributed fashion than would be typical for countries with more resources" (Nardi et al., 2018, p. 91), with survivors installing private water tanks and creating informal power-sharing arrangements.
- Research on networking in refugee camps in Zambia and Guatemala
- Studies of infrastructure in conditions of scarcity in Haiti
- Work on alternative decentralized infrastructures

**Evidence of Current LIMITS Scenarios:**
"To speak of LIMITS-scenarios only in the future tense, however, is misleading. These events are here now, as several climate-related catastrophes in the U.S. and Europe have shown" (Nardi et al., 2018, p. 91). The authors reference Flint, Michigan's water crisis and note the applicability to "heat waves, drought, rising sea levels, and floods" (Nardi et al., 2018, p. 91).

**Methodological Limitations:**

As a review article proposing a new research agenda, this work has several inherent limitations:

1. **Conceptual rather than empirical:** The article synthesizes existing research but does not present new empirical data testing LIMITS frameworks.

2. **Normative framing:** The authors openly advocate for a particular worldview about sustainability and growth, which some may view as ideological rather than purely scientific.

3. **Uncertainty about futures:** The authors acknowledge: "While we do not know for certain what the future holds, scientists from disciplines such as climate science and ecology have made evidence-based predictions about directions the future will likely take if current trends continue" (Nardi et al., 2018, p. 93).

4. **Limited practical guidance:** While proposing three key principles (question growth, consider models of scarcity, reduce energy and material consumption), the article acknowledges that "LIMITS research, once applied, should reduce energy expenditures and material consumption. This reduction is difficult to assess, but not something we can sidestep" (Nardi et al., 2018, p. 92).

5. **Tension with mainstream computing:** The authors note that "currently, the implicit organizing framework for a great deal of computing work puts a focus on increasing the proximate financial value of companies" (Nardi et al., 2018, p. 91), suggesting significant institutional barriers to LIMITS adoption.

## 6. Conclusion

**Synthesis for Future Recall:**

This 2018 article in Communications of the ACM represents a manifesto for reorienting computing research toward ecological sustainability. If you need to recall this paper in six months, remember these core elements:

**The Core Problem:** Most computing research implicitly assumes perpetual economic and technological growth - more users, more data, more processing power, more devices. But scientific evidence from ecology, climate science, and economics indicates humanity is hitting planetary limits. Computing researchers, by choosing what problems to work on and how to evaluate solutions, are implicitly predicting and shaping futures - currently futures of unlimited growth that may not materialize.

**The Proposed Solution - LIMITS:** The authors propose "computing within limits" as a new research paradigm integrating three concerns: (1) understanding current ecological/material/energy limits, (2) designing computing that supports human well-being within those limits, and (3) anticipating how these limits will affect computing itself. This shifts focus from productivity and growth metrics toward long-term well-being.

**Three Key Principles for LIMITS Research:**
1. **Question growth** - Explicitly consider whether your work relies on growth, seeks to make growth happen, or contributes to growth. Build systems that neither require nor contribute to runaway growth.
2. **Consider models of scarcity** - Rather than assuming technological fixes will defer catastrophes indefinitely, develop case studies of how communities adapt to resource constraints (like post-earthquake Haiti).
3. **Reduce energy and material consumption** - LIMITS research should demonstrably reduce resource use, accounting for complexities like the Jevons paradox where efficiency gains can increase total consumption.

**Intellectual Lineage:** The framework draws on Herman Daly's steady-state economics (qualitative development without quantitative growth), Joseph Tainter's archaeological analysis of societal collapse (complexity creates unsustainable burdens), and the planetary boundaries framework. It connects to existing computing subfields: Sustainable HCI, Crisis Informatics, and ICTD.

**Why This Matters for HCI/Computing Students:** The article challenges you to examine the assumptions embedded in your research questions. When you design a system to "scale" or "grow the user base" or "increase engagement," you're assuming a growth-oriented future. LIMITS asks: what if that future doesn't arrive? What if we designed for resilience, sufficiency, and distributed low-resource operation instead?

**Key Quote to Remember:** "We encourage those working in computing to build systems and envision worlds that are neither reliant on nor contributing to runaway growth" (p. 91).

**Practical Takeaway:** Before evaluating research by metrics of efficiency, scale, or adoption, consider whether it would help society function within ecological limits, maintain well-being during resource constraints, or reduce overall material/energy throughput.

## 8. Discussion Questions

1. **Examining Assumptions:** The authors argue that computing research implicitly assumes perpetual growth. Identify a current research project or technology trend (e.g., AI scaling, cryptocurrency, IoT) and analyze what assumptions about future resource availability and growth are embedded in it. How would the project need to change under LIMITS constraints?

2. **Tension Between ICTD and LIMITS:** The article notes a "tension between economic development in poor countries - the focus of ICTD - and sustainability." How can computing researchers ethically navigate the competing demands of (a) supporting economic development in the Global South and (b) reducing global resource consumption? Is there a path that satisfies both goals?

3. **Evaluating LIMITS Research:** The authors acknowledge that assessing whether research "reduces energy and material consumption" is difficult. What metrics or evaluation frameworks could the computing community develop to assess whether proposed systems genuinely operate within ecological limits versus merely shifting resource burdens elsewhere (e.g., to other countries or future generations)?

4. **The Role of Crisis:** The article draws connections between LIMITS and crisis informatics, noting that LIMITS scenarios (climate disasters, resource scarcity) are "here now." Should computing research prioritize adaptation to crises that are already occurring, or prevention of future crises? How does this priority affect what kinds of systems we build and for whom?

## 9. Bias Check

**Reflection on Neutrality:**

This article is explicitly normative and advocacy-oriented rather than neutrally descriptive. The authors openly argue for a particular worldview (that growth is problematic and computing must change) and propose a research agenda aligned with that view. This is appropriate for a position paper/manifesto but should be recognized as such.

**Potential Biases:**

1. **Disciplinary bias:** All seven authors are computing researchers invested in establishing LIMITS as a legitimate research area, which may lead to overstating computing's role in addressing ecological limits.

2. **Western academic perspective:** Despite international author affiliations, the framing reflects concerns and values more prominent in wealthy countries that have already industrialized.

3. **Techno-solutionist residue:** While critiquing technology's role in growth, the article still positions computing as central to solutions, potentially underestimating the role of political, economic, and social change.

4. **Selective evidence:** The synthesis draws heavily on sources supporting the limits thesis; counterarguments from economists who see growth as compatible with sustainability receive less attention.

5. **Catastrophism concerns:** Critics might argue the article adopts an overly pessimistic view of growth possibilities and technological innovation's potential to address limits.

**What the Article Does Well:**
- Transparently identifies its normative stance
- Draws on diverse scientific disciplines
- Acknowledges uncertainties about the future
- Connects to established HCI frameworks (SHCI)
- Provides concrete principles for action

**Accuracy Score: 7/10**

The article accurately represents the cited literature and the state of ecological economics and sustainability science. The synthesis of LIMITS workshops and related computing research is thorough. However, the score reflects: (1) the inherently speculative nature of claims about future scenarios, (2) limited engagement with counterarguments from growth-oriented economists, and (3) the difficulty of empirically validating the proposed LIMITS framework. The article is more persuasive as a call to action than as a comprehensive evidence review.
