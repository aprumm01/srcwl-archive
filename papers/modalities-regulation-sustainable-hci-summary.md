# Study Partner Summary: Modalities of Regulation in Sustainable HCI

## 1. Overview of the Document

This paper represents a significant theoretical contribution to the field of Sustainable Human-Computer Interaction (SHCI), published in the Proceedings of the 2026 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems held in Barcelona, Spain. The five-author team brings together an interdisciplinary combination of expertise spanning computer science, media technology, interaction design, and law.

Chiara Rossitto and Anton Poikolainen Rosen are affiliated with the Department of Computer and Systems Science at Stockholm University in Sweden. Their backgrounds in computer science and systems research position them to analyze the technical dimensions of digital platforms. Rob Comber and Fatemeh Bakhshoudeh represent the Department of Media Technology and Interaction Design at KTH Royal Institute of Technology, also in Stockholm, contributing expertise in how technology mediates human interaction and environmental practices. Perhaps most notably, Stanley J Greenstein from the Department of Law at Stockholm University provides essential legal scholarship that grounds the paper's regulatory framework analysis. This interdisciplinary composition is itself methodologically significant, as the paper argues that understanding technology regulation requires bridging legal, technical, and design perspectives.

The paper is a full-length ACM conference publication of 17 pages, licensed under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International License. It situates itself within the growing body of SHCI literature that calls for more ecological, systemic, and politically aware approaches to sustainability research. The authors explicitly respond to previous calls in the field for HCI to engage with sociopolitical, structural, historical, and geographical aspects of sustainability. By drawing on legal scholarship, particularly the work of Lawrence Lessig on regulation, the paper offers SHCI researchers a new analytical vocabulary for understanding how technology design intersects with law, markets, social norms, and code in shaping sustainable or unsustainable practices. The significance lies in moving beyond behavior-change paradigms toward understanding designers as regulators operating within complex sociotechnical systems.

## 2. Research Overview

The central research question driving this paper concerns how the concept of regulation, understood through multiple modalities beyond hard law, can help HCI researchers and practitioners develop more ecological and contextually situated accounts of digital technology for sustainable practices. The authors ask what it means to consider designers as regulators and what that reconceptualization entails for SHCI research and practice.

The methodological approach is primarily conceptual and analytical rather than empirical. The authors draw on previously published content analysis of legal proceedings between the waste management platform TipTapp and Stockholm Municipality. They complement this documentary analysis with an approximately one-hour audio-recorded interview with a senior employee at the TipTapp company, conducted almost two years after the court's final ruling. For the second case, Klimatkollen (a not-for-profit carbon emissions tracking platform), the understanding is based on explorations of the technology, repeated conversations with a co-founder, and analysis of the platform's public communications and design. The contribution is explicitly described as "conceptual rather than empirical," using the cases to illustrate theoretical points rather than to generate generalizable empirical findings.

The key concepts center on Lessig's four modalities of regulation: law, norms, market, and architecture (which the authors term "code" in digital contexts). As the paper articulates: "Lessig [46] argues that, while regulation is often associated with hard law and its function, social norms, architecture (e.g., interface design, code, network protocols), and markets also regulate behaviours, and other aspects of social life" (Rossitto et al., 2026, p. 3). The authors introduce additional analytical concepts including "doing historicism" (using historical context to interpret current regulations), "giving form" (how design gives structure to abstract legal requirements), and "datafication" (translating social life into quantified data for regulation). These concepts enable a more nuanced understanding of how digital technologies become entangled in regulatory processes that extend far beyond mere legal compliance.

## 3. Theories of Knowledge

The paper builds its theoretical foundation primarily on Lawrence Lessig's conceptualization of regulatory modalities, originally developed in his work on Internet regulation. Lessig's framework identifies four forces that constrain and enable human behavior: law, social norms, markets, and architecture. In digital environments, architecture translates to code and interface design. The paper explains that "the 'regulation' of this dot is essentially the interplay of these four constraints on the dot, where, in turn, a change to one of the modalities will affect the regulation of the dot as a whole" (Rossitto et al., 2026, p. 3). Critically, the modalities are not independent but mutually constitutive, supporting and contrasting one another dynamically.

The authors also draw on the notion of mediation from design theory, which holds that "technology is never neutral, and that it uniquely shapes and transforms activities by bringing together intentions, values, and other culturally and contextually specific aspects" (Rossitto et al., 2026, p. 3). This perspective from scholars like Latour and Verbeek positions technology design as inherently value-laden and politically consequential.

Additional theoretical resources include the distinction between hard law and soft law in legal scholarship. Hard law refers to legally binding rules with formal sanctions, while soft law encompasses "norms, codes of conduct, policies, recommendations, resolutions, and non-ratified conventions" that are not legally binding but still exercise regulatory influence (Rossitto et al., 2026, p. 4). The paper also engages with critical scholarship on datafication and its tendency toward "surveillance and monitoring" as critiqued by Dourish, and with Paul Dourish's concept of ecotopanism. These theoretical threads weave together to support the paper's argument that design is always already a form of regulation that must be understood within its specific legal, market, normative, and infrastructural context.

## 4. Central Arguments

The paper advances three interconnected arguments that collectively reframe how SHCI should understand design and sustainability.

The first and most fundamental argument is that design should be understood as a form of regulation. The authors contend that "code can be considered as the most effective form of regulation, and we emphasise that this is true both when code is considered independently or through its inter-dependencies with the other modalities" (Rossitto et al., 2026, p. 12). This claim positions designers, data scientists, and systems engineers as regulators whose work shapes what behaviors are possible, encouraged, or constrained. The regulatory function of design operates through what the authors call architecture or code modality, which "outlines that technology itself regulates behaviours by design, either by constraining or enabling certain actions and activities" (Rossitto et al., 2026, p. 3).

The second argument concerns the dynamic and interdependent nature of regulatory modalities. Rather than treating law, markets, norms, and code as separate forces, the paper demonstrates through its case studies how these modalities become "locally and temporally entangled in, and through, technology design" (Rossitto et al., 2026, p. 3). In the TipTapp case, the legal dispute was not simply about law but about how legal interpretations interacted with market mechanisms (the gig economy model for waste transport), social norms (expectations about waste disposal responsibility), and code (the platform's verification features). Similarly, Klimatkollen operates at the intersection of EU sustainability reporting directives (law), investor and consumer pressure (market), public shaming of high-emission companies (norms), and data visualization design choices (code).

The third argument addresses the specific analytical dimensions that help SHCI researchers engage with regulatory modalities: relations to relevant legal landscapes, reactive and proactive relations with regulations, historical relations, and political relations. The paper argues that "identifying relevant laws, interpreting them, or predicting the consequences of non-compliance (where this is more immediately visible) might require legal expertise, designers can regulate through soft laws" (Rossitto et al., 2026, p. 13). This suggests that while designers may not be legal experts, they exercise regulatory agency through how they standardize data, create accountability mechanisms, and translate abstract legal requirements into concrete design features.

## 5. Evidence

The paper draws its primary evidence from two Swedish case studies that illustrate contrasting relationships between technology platforms and regulatory modalities.

The TipTapp case provides rich evidence of how law, markets, norms, and code interact dynamically over time. TipTapp is a gig-work platform connecting people who want to dispose of bulky household waste with "helpers" who transport it for compensation. The platform became legally contested when Stockholm Municipality sued, arguing it violated national laws giving municipalities monopoly control over waste management. The evidence shows how the company's legal team deployed historical interpretation, tracing waste management law back to the 1970s Sanitation Act to argue that current laws were "obsolete and regulate different circumstances" (Rossitto et al., 2026, p. 7). The court case outcome, ultimately favoring TipTapp, demonstrates how "digital innovation stemming from the bottom up can legally be appropriated at scale, despite tensions with local governance" (Rossitto et al., 2026, p. 6). A particularly illuminating piece of evidence concerns the platform's design evolution: after media reports of improperly dumped waste, TipTapp added a QR code verification feature requiring photographic proof of proper disposal with GPS coordinates. This design change demonstrates how architecture (code) can be deployed to respond to regulatory pressure from norms (public criticism) and law (municipal concerns about environmental protection).

The Klimatkollen case provides evidence of a different regulatory configuration. This not-for-profit platform scrapes publicly available corporate sustainability reports, standardizes the fragmented data, and visualizes companies' carbon emissions in an accessible format. The platform's work is "tied not to laws regulating sustainability or environmental protection, but to the financial reporting of companies" (Rossitto et al., 2026, p. 7). The EU's Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) mandates that companies report environmental data but does not specify reporting format, creating what the authors call an "interpretative gap" that Klimatkollen fills through design. Evidence from platform analysis and conversations with the co-founder reveals how the organization strategically uses media attention to exert market pressure on companies, aligning with "market-oriented notions of being an informed consumer and citizen, even when acting as an investor" (Rossitto et al., 2026, p. 10).

The methodological limitations are acknowledged. The authors did not interview the municipality officials in the TipTapp legal dispute, potentially creating a partial account. They chose not to anonymize the cases because "their names convey meanings about their roles and purposes that would be lost through anonymisation" (Rossitto et al., 2026, p. 6), though this raises questions about generalizability. The conceptual rather than empirical contribution means the analysis illustrates theoretical points without systematic data collection across multiple cases. The Swedish context, with its specific legal traditions and social-democratic governance structures, may limit transferability to other regulatory environments.

## 6. Conclusion

This paper makes a distinctive contribution to Sustainable HCI by importing and adapting Lawrence Lessig's regulatory modalities framework to analyze how digital technology design intersects with law, markets, social norms, and code in shaping environmental practices. Through two Swedish case studies (TipTapp's waste transportation platform and Klimatkollen's corporate emissions tracker), the authors demonstrate that regulation is not merely something external that designers must comply with, but rather something that design itself enacts through its architectural choices.

The key takeaway for recall six months from now is the reconceptualization of designers as regulators. When you create a digital platform, you are not simply building a tool but constructing a regulatory architecture that enables certain behaviors, constrains others, and operates in dynamic tension with laws, markets, and social norms. The TipTapp case shows how a platform's design features (like QR code verification) become regulatory mechanisms that reshape waste management practices and municipal governance. Klimatkollen demonstrates how data visualization design can translate abstract legal reporting requirements into public accountability tools that leverage market pressure.

For your I609 course on Sustainability, Resilience, and Computing within Limits, this paper offers several critical insights. First, it challenges the behavior-change paradigm that dominates much sustainability technology research by situating individual choices within broader regulatory structures. Second, it provides analytical vocabulary (the four modalities, plus concepts like "doing historicism" and "giving form") for examining how computing within limits involves navigation of legal, economic, and normative constraints. Third, it makes the political dimensions of design visible, arguing that "technologies are political tools as they reflect norms and values, power relations, and economic forces that inevitably shape what can be done with them, by whom, and how" (Rossitto et al., 2026, p. 13).

The paper's most provocative implication is that SHCI researchers and practitioners should develop what the authors call "localising sensitivity for design, to avoid globalising or homogenising tendencies, to think and act locally first" (Rossitto et al., 2026, p. 12). Sustainability is not achieved through universal design solutions but through contextually sensitive interventions that understand and work within specific regulatory configurations. This aligns with limits-aware computing's emphasis on situatedness, materiality, and the political economy of technological systems.

## 8. Discussion Questions

**Question 1:** The authors argue that designers are regulators whose work shapes behavior as powerfully as laws or markets. What are the ethical implications of this framing for HCI practitioners who may not have legal training or democratic accountability? Should there be mechanisms for public input into design decisions that have regulatory effects?

**Question 2:** The paper focuses on Swedish cases embedded in a social-democratic welfare state with strong environmental regulations and EU membership. How might the interplay of regulatory modalities differ in contexts with weaker environmental law, different property regimes, or authoritarian governance? What does this suggest about the limits of design-as-regulation in achieving global sustainability goals?

**Question 3:** Klimatkollen uses public shaming and market pressure to influence corporate behavior, leveraging the "norms" modality to compensate for weak legal enforcement of environmental reporting standards. What are the risks of this approach? Could design that weaponizes social norms for sustainability also be used for less benign purposes?

**Question 4:** The paper positions code/architecture as potentially "the most effective form of regulation." Given the increasing use of AI systems in environmental governance and sustainability platforms, how should we think about algorithmic regulation? When AI systems make decisions about resource allocation or environmental compliance, who is the regulator and where does accountability lie?

## 9. Bias Check

**Neutrality Reflection:** The paper demonstrates several potential biases that readers should consider. First, the author team is entirely based in Swedish institutions, and both case studies are Swedish, creating a Nordic-centric perspective on regulation that may not generalize to other legal and cultural contexts. The authors acknowledge this limitation but do not systematically address it. Second, the paper takes a broadly critical stance toward market-driven sustainability solutions while remaining sympathetic to regulatory intervention, reflecting what appears to be a social-democratic political orientation. Third, the TipTapp case is analyzed primarily from the company's perspective (via an employee interview), with municipal perspectives derived only from court documents. Fourth, the theoretical commitment to Lessig's framework, while productive, may obscure alternative conceptualizations of regulation from other scholarly traditions.

The paper does not engage substantively with counterarguments that might favor deregulation or market-based environmental solutions. The authors' claim that "we hope such endeavours can be legally monitored and sanctioned, when needed" (Rossitto et al., 2026, p. 6) reveals a normative preference for legal oversight of technology that is presented as analytical observation rather than argued position.

**Accuracy Score: 8/10**

The paper is methodologically transparent about its limitations, carefully documents its sources, and presents its cases with appropriate nuance. The theoretical framework is rigorously applied and well-cited. Points deducted for: the single-perspective limitation in the TipTapp case (-1), and the potential conflation of analytical claims with normative preferences about what sustainability regulation should look like (-1). The paper succeeds at what it sets out to do, namely providing a conceptual contribution illustrated by cases, but readers should be aware that the empirical foundation is selective rather than comprehensive.
