# Four Epistemic Traditions of Evaluation

**Study Partner Summary for I609**

## Overview of the Document

Peter Gall Krogh is a design researcher whose work focuses on constructive design research and design methodology. Ilpo Koskinen is a professor who has written extensively about design research methods, including the influential text "Design Research Through Practice." Together, they are experts in understanding how design produces knowledge and how that knowledge should be evaluated. This chapter comes from their book *Drifting by Intention: Four Epistemic Traditions of Evaluation* published by Springer in 2020.

This is a theoretical framework chapter that maps the landscape of evaluation practices in constructive design research. The chapter addresses a fundamental challenge: how do we judge whether a theory produced through design is useful, valuable, or successful? The authors argue that evaluation must be understood through the lens of epistemology, meaning the evaluation criteria that apply depend on the underlying tradition of knowledge production a researcher works within.

The chapter is significant because design research has long struggled with questions of validity and rigor. Some scholars argue design should adopt scientific evaluation standards, while others argue it should develop its own criteria. Krogh and Koskinen take a different route by showing that evaluation already happens in multiple legitimate ways depending on one's epistemic commitments. This allows researchers to be accountable within their chosen tradition rather than being held to inappropriate standards from another tradition.

---

## Research Overview

The central research question asks: on what grounds do we judge whether a theory for design is useful, valuable, or successful? The authors do not conduct new empirical research but instead analyze existing design research dissertations and projects to build a typology of evaluation practices.

Their claim is that "evaluation takes different forms depending on the underlying epistemological tradition" (Krogh & Koskinen, 2020, p. 73). This means the yardstick for measuring contributions varies between traditions because each tradition holds different concepts of knowledge as central.

The chapter identifies four epistemic traditions: the experiential tradition, where evaluation happens through probing subjective responses to designed objects; the methodic tradition, where evaluation uses controlled experiments and accumulative methods from science; the programmatic tradition, where evaluation happens through comparison to discourse and previous research; and the dialectic tradition, where evaluation is anchored in community response and engagement.

Key concepts include "probing" (collecting subjective opinions about designed objects), "accumulative" methods (building knowledge by layering experiments around a fixed nucleus of criteria), and "performative objects" (designed artifacts that prompt mindfulness through disruption of function).

---

## Theories of Knowledge

The chapter's theoretical foundation rests on the idea that design research can be understood through different epistemic traditions, each with its own standards for what counts as valid knowledge. The authors draw on the K-R model presented in earlier chapters of their book to map how these traditions relate to theory and practice.

In the experiential tradition, knowledge emerges from subjective encounters with designed objects. The authors cite practices "akin to showroom methodology" (Koskinen et al., 2011) where "a constructive design researcher cherishes the fact that people form their opinion of a design object differently from hers" (Krogh & Koskinen, 2020, p. 74). Evaluation criteria here include novelty, surprise, and skill in execution rather than scientific rigor.

In the methodic tradition, "design is seen as a science" (p. 76) and researchers must create theoretical models, conduct experiments, and compare findings to the model. The authors describe this as "accumulative" because "the understanding of every outcome is gained only by falling back into the center" (p. 76) of a fixed nucleus of criteria.

In the programmatic tradition, following John Zimmerman and colleagues, "evaluation can be done against the discourse behind research projects" (p. 77). Success is measured by whether a study contributes something new to previous research.

In the dialectic tradition, "the community is the anchor" (p. 79) and evaluation happens through how target communities respond to design interventions.

---

## Central Arguments

The central argument is that constructive design research has developed a mature set of evaluation practices, but these practices only make sense when understood within their respective epistemic traditions. The authors state: "we have argued that contributions in constructive design research is a matter of to whom and what the research is held accountable to, and a key measure of accountability is how and what you chose to evaluate" (Krogh & Koskinen, 2020, p. 80).

A key sub-claim is that diversity in evaluation is actually a strength, not a weakness. The authors note that "this diversity can also be seen as a sign of maturity" (p. 73) because design researchers have developed approaches suited to different kinds of knowledge production.

Another important claim is that researchers can strategically position themselves within traditions. By "knowing their preferred epistemic tradition, they can evaluate their work in ways that anticipate the expectations of their audience" (p. 81). This means evaluation is not merely about meeting abstract standards but about communicating effectively with specific research communities.

The authors also argue against importing evaluation methods wholesale from other disciplines. While some scholars call for "adapting more rigorous methods from science, technology, social science and the humanities" (Zimmerman et al., 2010), others "deconstruct the idea of rigor with philosophical and sometimes artistic references" (Gaver, 2012). The four traditions framework provides a middle path that respects both positions.

## Evidence

The authors support their framework through detailed analysis of doctoral dissertations and design projects. For the experiential tradition, they examine three dissertations: Kart Summatavet's *Folk Tradition and Artistic Inspiration* (2005), von Busch's *FASHION-able* (2008), and Maarit Mäkelä's *Memories on Clay* (2003).

Summatavet's work explored jewelry in three stages of a woman's life. Her "design objects were exquisitely crafted" and "juried by professional goldsmiths, exhibited publicly in high-end museums" (p. 74). Importantly, she "consistently separated her field studies and art work, and refused to show the connection" (p. 74), treating the art itself as the main evaluation criterion.

Von Busch's dissertation focused on "hacktivism" in fashion, including "a failed attempt to redesign a Turkish fashion firm, and establishing a student magazine" (p. 74). His evaluation criteria were whether DIY methods could democratize fashion, not whether the interventions met scientific standards of success.

For the methodic tradition, the authors examine Philip Ross's work on intelligent lamp design using three perspectives: Dynamic Form, Social Activity, and Sensor-Motoric Activity. Ross "deliberately chose neutral colors, material and a static shape to rule out any influence from factors other than those of interest" (p. 76), demonstrating controlled experimental methods.

For the dialectic tradition, they analyze Christian Dindler's museum installations at Kattegat Marine Centre and Moesgaard Museum, where evaluation was based on community engagement rather than the designer's intentions.

A methodological limitation is that the chapter draws primarily on European design research traditions, particularly Scandinavian and Dutch programs. The framework may not fully capture evaluation practices in other contexts.

---

## Conclusion

This chapter makes an important contribution by showing that design research does not lack evaluation standards; rather, it has multiple legitimate evaluation traditions that serve different knowledge production goals. For students in I609, this framework helps resolve a common confusion: why do some design papers read like scientific reports while others read like artist statements? The answer is that they operate in different epistemic traditions with different validity criteria.

The practical takeaway is that researchers should be explicit about their epistemic commitments and evaluate accordingly. If you work in the experiential tradition, your evaluation criteria should emphasize craft, novelty, and subjective response. If you work in the methodic tradition, you need controlled experiments and transparent methods. If programmatic, you must position your contribution against existing discourse. If dialectic, community engagement and impact matter most.

For sustainability and limits computing research, this framework suggests multiple valid approaches. A collapse informatics project could be evaluated through experiential criteria (does the speculative artifact provoke meaningful reflection?), methodic criteria (does the system perform as hypothesized under resource constraints?), programmatic criteria (does it advance discourse on post-growth computing?), or dialectic criteria (does a community actually adopt and benefit from it?). Recognizing these options allows researchers to choose evaluation methods suited to their specific contributions.

Six months from now, remember: evaluation in design research is epistemically plural. The question is not "is this rigorous?" but "rigorous by whose standards and for what purpose?"

## Discussion Questions

1. The authors argue evaluation should match epistemic tradition. But what happens when a design research project genuinely spans multiple traditions? Can you evaluate the same project as both experiential and methodic, or must you choose?

2. The dialectic tradition evaluates design by community response. How might this apply to sustainability computing, where the relevant "community" might be future generations who cannot participate in current evaluation?

3. Von Busch's hacktivism dissertation included a "failed attempt to redesign a Turkish fashion firm." Under what conditions should failure count as valid research contribution, and how do different epistemic traditions handle negative results?

4. The authors suggest researchers should choose evaluation approaches that "anticipate the expectations of their audience." Is this pragmatic wisdom or does it risk becoming strategic game-playing that undermines genuine knowledge production?
