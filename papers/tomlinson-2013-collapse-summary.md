This is a theoretical framework paper published in ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction in 2013. It represents a significant contribution to sustainable HCI by introducing an entirely new research domain. The paper bridges multiple fields including archaeology, sociology, environmental studies, and computer science to argue for a fundamental shift in how the HCI community thinks about the future.

---

## Research Overview

The central research question asks: How should the HCI community prepare for the possibility that global industrial civilization may experience significant decline or collapse within the coming decades? The authors do not conduct new empirical research but instead synthesize scholarship from archaeology, environmental science, practice theory, and sustainable HCI to construct a theoretical framework and research agenda.

The paper defines collapse informatics as "the study, design, and development of sociotechnical systems in the abundant present for use in a future of scarcity" (Tomlinson et al., 2013, p. 24:2). This definition captures the core paradox the authors address: designing systems now, in conditions of relative abundance, for deployment in radically different future conditions characterized by resource constraints.

Key concepts include collapse itself, which the authors define following archaeologist Joseph Tainter as "a rapid, significant loss of an established level of sociopolitical complexity" (Tainter, 1990, p. 4). They distinguish this from apocalyptic scenarios, noting that historical collapses typically unfold over decades rather than days. They also introduce "purposefully marginal groups" and "technofuturists" as populations whose current practices may provide insight into collapse-ready lifestyles.

---

## Theories of Knowledge

The paper's theoretical foundation rests primarily on practice theory, a sociological framework with roots in the work of Pierre Bourdieu, Anthony Giddens, and Theodore Schatzki. Practice theory understands human activity as "materially mediated arrays of human activity centrally organized around shared practical understanding" (Schatzki, 2001, p. 2). This framework emphasizes that practices are collective, embodied, and mediated by material artifacts rather than being purely individual or cognitive.

The authors draw on Reckwitz's characterization of practices as "body/knowledge/things-complexes" (Reckwitz, 2002, p. 258) and Shove and Pantzar's formulation that "practices involve the active integration of materials, meanings and forms of competence" (Shove & Pantzar, 2005, p. 44). These definitions position practice theory as particularly suited to HCI research because it foregrounds the role of designed artifacts in shaping human activity.

A second theoretical thread comes from Marx Wartofsky's concept of "tertiary artifacts," which the authors update to "possible-worlds artifacts." Primary artifacts enable everyday tasks (a word processor for writing), secondary artifacts explain primary ones (documentation), and tertiary artifacts "transcend the more immediate necessities of productive praxis" to give "freer rein to imagining possible worlds" (Wartofsky, 1979, p. 79). The authors argue that collapse informatics should produce such tertiary artifacts that help people envision alternative futures.

---

## Central Arguments

The paper's central argument is that the HCI community has both the opportunity and responsibility to begin designing sociotechnical systems for potential futures of scarcity while resources remain abundant. The authors state: "As members of the HCI community we have a tremendous opportunity to impact the future through digital design aimed at everyday practice" (Tomlinson et al., 2013, p. 24:5).

This argument rests on several sub-claims. First, collapse is not merely a theoretical possibility but a historically documented pattern. The authors cite Diamond (2004) and Tainter (1990) to establish that "most civilizations that have ever existed have collapsed" (p. 24:1). Second, environmental indicators suggest contemporary industrial civilization may already be entering a period of decline. Third, ICT systems are deeply intertwined with industrial infrastructure and will be significantly affected by resource constraints. Fourth, practice theory provides a useful analytical lens because it focuses on collective activity mediated by material artifacts, exactly the domain where HCI researchers can intervene.

The authors argue that practice theory requires three methodological adjustments for collapse informatics: expanding temporal horizons beyond the typical "here-and-now" focus; conducting comparative research across diverse contexts to understand multiple possible futures; and continuously "checkpointing" findings against evolving conditions because the future remains unpredictable.

---

## Evidence

The authors support their arguments through several types of evidence. They cite archaeological and historical scholarship extensively, particularly Tainter's The Collapse of Complex Societies (1990) and Diamond's Collapse: How Societies Choose to Fail or Succeed (2004), to establish that civilizational collapse is a documented historical phenomenon rather than pure speculation.

Environmental evidence comes from IPCC reports indicating the world may reach climate tipping points by 2040 unless emissions are dramatically reduced, and from analysis of peak oil suggesting "declining returns for our reliance on fossil fuels" (Tainter, 1990, p. 215). The authors also cite military strategic planning documents that take collapse scenarios seriously, noting that Nathan Frier of the US Army War College Strategic Studies Institute has written about "the swift catastrophic onset of consequential natural, environmental, and/or human disaster" (Freier, 2008).

For practice theory's applicability, they point to empirical studies of "bright green" families and ecovillage communities who "were constantly thinking deliberatively about action in relation to their homes" (Woodruff et al., 2008). These groups demonstrate that reflexive, collapse-aware practice already exists and can be studied.

The paper's primary methodological limitation is that it presents a theoretical framework and research agenda rather than empirical findings. The authors acknowledge this, positioning the paper as "a call for research" rather than a report of completed research. They also note the inherent difficulty of designing for unknowable future conditions.

---

## Conclusion

This paper makes a significant contribution by carving out collapse informatics as a legitimate research domain within HCI. The core insight is that designers must begin thinking now about how digital technologies can support human flourishing in conditions of reduced complexity, even though such futures remain uncertain. Practice theory provides useful analytical tools because it focuses attention on collective, materially-mediated activities that can be shaped through design.

For students in I609, this paper establishes foundational vocabulary and concepts for thinking about computing within limits. The framework of "purposefully marginal groups" and "technofuturists" provides concrete starting points for empirical research. The three methodological adjustments to practice theory (expanded temporal scope, comparative research, checkpointing) offer practical guidance for conducting such research.

The paper's call to develop "possible-worlds artifacts" resonates with design research methods that use speculative prototypes to explore alternative futures. Six months from now, you should remember: collapse informatics is about designing for scarcity while resources are still abundant, practice theory provides the analytical framework, and the key populations to study are those already living outside mainstream consumption patterns.

## Discussion Questions

1. The paper was published in 2013. How have developments in AI, climate change, and global supply chains since then validated or complicated its predictions about collapse timelines?

2. The authors identify "purposefully marginal groups" and "technofuturists" as key populations for study. What are the ethical implications of treating these communities as research subjects for collapse preparedness? Does this risk appropriating their practices?

3. How do we balance designing for collapse (which some might see as defeatist or accelerationist) with efforts to prevent collapse through sustainability initiatives? Are these goals in tension?

4. What would a "tertiary artifact" or "possible-worlds artifact" look like in today's AI ecosystem? How might generative AI tools help or hinder people's ability to imagine alternative futures?
