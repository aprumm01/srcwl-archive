# Study-Partner Summary: Hope for the Best and Prepare for the Worst: Interaction Design and the Tipping Point

---

## 1. Overview of the Document

**Authors and Affiliations:**
- **Eli Blevis** - Associate Professor of Informatics in the Human-Computer Interaction Design program at the School of Informatics and Computing at Indiana University, Bloomington. His primary research area is sustainable interaction design, situated at the confluence of human-computer interaction, computing and cognitive sciences, design criticism, and critical design. His research also engages design theory, digital photography, and studio-based learning.
- **Shunying Blevis** - Independent scholar and social entrepreneur based in Bloomington, Indiana. She holds advanced degrees in informatics from Indiana University and Beijing Normal University. Her interests and practice include design, locavorism, and permaculture.

**Type of Document:**
This is a peer-reviewed forum article published in *interactions* magazine (ACM), appearing in the "Sustainably Ours" column during September-October 2010. The article is part of a special section titled "The Complexity of 'Advancement.'" Eli Blevis serves as the editor of this forum section, which focuses on sustainability issues within the field of human-computer interaction (HCI).

**Field and Significance:**
This article sits at the intersection of interaction design, sustainability studies, and climate science communication. It represents a significant contribution to the emerging field of sustainable interaction design (also known as sustainable HCI), which examines how digital technologies and interactive systems can be designed to support environmental sustainability. The paper is notable for its direct engagement with climate science, particularly the concept of the "tipping point" as articulated by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) and climate scientists such as James Lovelock, James Hansen, and Freeman Dyson. The significance of this work lies in its attempt to bridge the gap between the certainties of scientific predictions about climate change and the uncertainties that pervade public understanding, arguing that interaction design has a unique role to play in raising consciousness, changing behaviors, and preparing societies for climate adaptation. The article appeared at a crucial moment in the discourse around sustainable HCI, contributing to discussions about the discipline's responsibility to address global environmental challenges.

## 2. Research Overview

**Central Research Question:**
The paper addresses a fundamental question: How should interaction designers respond to the possibility (or likelihood) that we will reach a climate tipping point, and what role can digital interactivity play in both preventing climate change and preparing for its effects? The authors frame this through the logic of "hoping for the best and preparing for the worst" - a strategic approach that acknowledges uncertainty while demanding action.

**Who Conducted the Study:**
This is a conceptual and argumentative piece rather than an empirical study. Eli Blevis, with contributions from Shunying Blevis, synthesizes existing climate science research (particularly from the IPCC), theoretical frameworks from the Gaia hypothesis (James Lovelock), and the emerging literature on sustainable interaction design to make their case for a dual-pronged approach to interaction design practice.

**Data and Methods:**
The paper employs a synthesis methodology, drawing on: (1) the 2007 IPCC Fourth Assessment Report on climate change impacts, adaptation, and vulnerability; (2) James Lovelock's work on the Gaia hypothesis, particularly his book *The Vanishing Face of Gaia: A Final Warning*; (3) existing scholarship in sustainable HCI, including work by DiSalvo, Sengers, and Brynjarsdottir, as well as Tony Fry's work on design futuring; (4) examples of existing sustainable interaction design projects. The authors use this synthesis to construct a conceptual framework (presented as Figure 1) that maps different strategies against different climate outcomes.

**Key Concepts Defined:**
The authors define several critical concepts including the "tipping point" in climate terms, sustainable interaction design, and the relationship between mitigation and adaptation strategies. They distinguish between efforts to reduce greenhouse gas emissions (mitigation) and efforts to prepare for climate change effects (adaptation).

**Direct Verbatim Quote:**
The authors quote the IPCC directly on the tipping point concept: "*There is high confidence that neither adaptation nor mitigation alone can avoid all climate change impacts; however, they can complement each other and together can significantly reduce the risks of climate change. Adaptation is necessary in the short and longer term to address impacts resulting from the warming that would occur even for the lowest stabilization scenarios assessed. There are barriers, limits and costs, but these are not fully understood. Unmitigated climate change would, in the long term, be likely to exceed the capacity of natural, managed, and human systems to adapt. The time at which such limits could be reached will vary between sectors and regions...*" (Blevis & Blevis, 2010, p. 27, citing IPCC, 2007).

## 3. Theories of Knowledge

**Key Theoretical Frameworks:**

**The Gaia Hypothesis:**
Central to the authors' argument is James Lovelock's Gaia hypothesis, which posits that the Earth is a "living self-regulating system" (Blevis & Blevis, 2010, p. 27). The authors note that Lovelock "provides a mathematical model called 'Daisy World' to support his notion of Gaia - a model that will likely seem familiar and convincing to computer scientists and mathematically trained interaction designers" (p. 27). This theoretical framework positions climate systems as interconnected and self-regulating, which has implications for how intervention through design might work or fail.

**Connectionism:**
The authors reference connectionist theory from cognitive science, noting that "the notion of an emergence of apparently intelligent or self-regulating behaviors from myriad autonomous agents with highly variant degrees of sentience is easily grasped by computer scientists and interaction designers who may be familiar with connectionism as understood, for example, in Minsky's *The Society of Mind*" (Blevis & Blevis, 2010, p. 27).

**Sustainment vs. Sustainability:**
The paper draws on Tony Fry's distinction between sustainability and sustainment: "As a more general account of sustainable design, Tony Fry's notions of redirective practice, acts of elimination, and development of sustainment are keys to understanding sustainability not just as a matter of reducing greenhouse gas emissions, but more important, as a matter of adopting lifestyles that rely on fewer and more natural uses of resources" (Blevis & Blevis, 2010, p. 28).

**Design Problem-Setting:**
The authors invoke a "characteristic fashion of design thinking" (p. 29) that emphasizes problem-setting over problem-solving. This approach suggests that interaction designers should be proposing new types of systems rather than simply optimizing existing ones.

**All Terminology with APA Citations:**
- Tipping point: "The tipping point, by definition, is the point at which any efforts to stop something from happening arrive too late" (Blevis & Blevis, 2010, p. 26).
- Gaia hypothesis: Earth as "a living self-regulating system" (Blevis & Blevis, 2010, p. 27).
- Sustainable interaction design: Design concerned with "inducing behavioral changes that reduce our greenhouse gas emissions" (Blevis & Blevis, 2010, p. 26).

## 4. Central Arguments

**Central Argument:**
The central argument of this paper is that interaction designers must pursue a dual strategy: continuing efforts to reduce greenhouse gas emissions through behavioral change while simultaneously developing interactive systems that help people prepare for and adapt to climate change impacts. The authors state this explicitly: "We are at a crossroads where the logic that applies is the logic of hoping for the best and preparing for the worst - that is, we need to continue our efforts to develop digital interactivity to induce people and societies to engage in behavioral changes that reduce our greenhouse gas emissions. We also need to develop digital interactivity that allows us to prepare for and adapt to climate change under the possibility that we will reach a tipping point, or the possibility that we have already done so" (Blevis & Blevis, 2010, p. 26).

The authors emphasize that "interaction design is a science, and an art, of compassion and that saving lives above all else and incorporating sustainability whenever possible is a suitable and critical goal for our discipline - resigning to Lovelock's prediction of the potential loss of roughly six out of every seven people and many species is not an option" (Blevis & Blevis, 2010, p. 29).

**Key Sub-Claims:**

1. **Consciousness-raising is essential:** "Typical interaction designers are not climate scientists, but interaction designers can make well-informed use of climate sciences and closely related sciences. Interaction design can make scientific information, interpretations, and perspectives available in an accessible and widely distributed form so that people's consciousness is raised. Such consciousness raising is our only hope to empower individuals, groups, nations, and the international community to possibly prevent or reverse climate change" (Blevis & Blevis, 2010, p. 26).

2. **Both mitigation and adaptation are necessary:** The authors argue that sustainable interaction design has focused primarily on changing behaviors to reduce emissions, but must also focus on adaptation: "from the logic of 'hope for the best and prepare for the worst,' it is clear that at least some if not many of us need to also consider the degree to which some of our efforts need to be directed toward designing digital interactivity to prepare for and adapt to the potential effects of global warming and climate change" (Blevis & Blevis, 2010, p. 28).

3. **Design must address multiple domains:** The paper identifies specific areas where interaction design interventions are needed: food production and source tracking, "dashboard earth" monitoring systems, orderly absorption and evacuation planning, habitability indexes, living with fewer resources, saving life through social mechanisms, fostering public and governmental support for technological solutions, and fostering public understanding.

4. **Computing issues are part of sustainability:** The authors note that "the focus of attention on computing issues has centered on issues of energy use, including individual (personal computers), corporate (servers), and very large-scale (cloud- and grid-computing) levels" (Blevis & Blevis, 2010, p. 30), but this is only part of what is needed.

## 5. Evidence

**Evidence Supporting Arguments:**

The authors marshal several types of evidence to support their arguments:

**Scientific Authority (IPCC):**
The 2007 Nobel Prize-winning IPCC report serves as primary evidence for the urgency of the climate situation. The authors cite that "according to the 2007 Nobel Prize-winning Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), we are at imminent risk of reaching a tipping point with respect to climate change and beyond the point at which even the very best case scenarios predict a certain unavoidable amount of climate change" (Blevis & Blevis, 2010, p. 26). The IPCC report also provides evidence about specific impacts: "The 2007 IPCC report predicts climate change will have massive implications for global food production and conditions of production, as well as for water, coastal habitations, health, and ecosystems" (Blevis & Blevis, 2010, p. 29).

**Expert Scientific Opinion:**
The paper engages with multiple climate scientists' perspectives. Regarding Lovelock's predictions: "He believes we have already passed the tipping point - the point at which positive-feedback mechanisms will induce the near certain likelihood of a period of global warming, regardless of our efforts to reform our behaviors. That is, global warming will continue even if all anthropogenic greenhouse gas production ceases" (Blevis & Blevis, 2010, p. 27). The authors also note that "James Hansen's and Freeman Dyson's views are at opposite sides of the debate about climate change" (p. 27), acknowledging scientific disagreement while arguing this disagreement itself justifies their dual approach.

**Existing Design Practice:**
The authors reference existing sustainable HCI projects as evidence of what the field can accomplish: "There are some substantial projects, too numerous to inventory here - examples include software for showing the source of constituent elements of products for small-business owners, data mining by SAP to track carbon as if it were a commodity, and software targeted at letting individuals understand and take steps to lessen their carbon footprints" (Blevis & Blevis, 2010, p. 28). They also mention "StepGreen (http://www.stepgreen.org/). Dan Rosenberg and Daniela Busse from SAP are building dashboards to track carbon based on the considerable scale and reach of SAP software installations" (p. 28).

**Logical Framework:**
Figure 1 provides a logical matrix mapping strategies (reduce emissions/find solutions, reduce emissions and prepare for effects, prepare for effects) against conditions (tipping point not yet reached, tipping point already passed), showing outcomes ranging from "we're saved" to "we've prepared as best we can."

**Methodological Limitations:**

1. **Conceptual rather than empirical:** This paper does not present empirical evidence about whether interaction design interventions actually change behaviors or improve adaptation. The authors acknowledge this gap, noting "there is much left to do and to write" (p. 28).

2. **Uncertainty about climate scenarios:** The authors acknowledge they are "not qualified to do so" (p. 27) when it comes to entering debates about scientific predictions, and they note "we are taking Lovelock's view to delimit another view - a view that is both worst case and plausible" (p. 27).

3. **Limited scope of solutions:** The authors present a design problem-setting approach rather than fully developed solutions, stating "what follows are some speculations about the kinds of things the interaction design community can be doing" (p. 29).

4. **Reliance on secondary sources:** Much of the climate science is mediated through Lovelock's interpretations rather than direct engagement with primary scientific literature.

## 6. Conclusion

**Synthesis for Future Recall:**

This 2010 article by Eli and Shunying Blevis represents a pivotal contribution to sustainable interaction design (sustainable HCI) by arguing that the field must adopt a dual strategy in response to climate change: continuing efforts to reduce emissions through behavior change while simultaneously developing systems to help humanity adapt to climate impacts that may be unavoidable.

The key takeaway for students is the "hope for the best, prepare for the worst" framework. This framework acknowledges that we cannot know with certainty whether humanity will avert catastrophic climate change, but this uncertainty does not excuse inaction. Instead, it demands action on multiple fronts. Interaction designers, according to the Blevises, have unique capabilities to bridge the gap between scientific knowledge and public understanding, and to create tools that support both mitigation and adaptation.

The paper identifies eight specific domains where interaction design can contribute: (1) food production and source tracking systems; (2) "dashboard earth" environmental monitoring; (3) orderly absorption and evacuation planning for climate refugees; (4) habitability indexes for regions; (5) systems for living with fewer resources; (6) social mechanisms for saving life; (7) fostering public and governmental support for technological solutions; and (8) fostering public understanding of energy production trade-offs.

Theoretically, the paper draws on Lovelock's Gaia hypothesis (Earth as self-regulating system), connectionist theories from cognitive science, and Tony Fry's concept of sustainment. These frameworks position the Earth's climate as a complex adaptive system that design interventions must respect.

For seminar discussion, remember that this paper sits at a historical moment (2010) when sustainable HCI was emerging as a recognized subfield. The authors are making a normative claim about what interaction designers should do, grounded in their interpretation of climate science. The paper's strength is its willingness to engage directly with uncomfortable possibilities (mass human die-off) while maintaining that design can and must respond constructively. Its limitation is that it offers a conceptual framework rather than empirical evidence about what works.

The authors ultimately argue that "interaction design is a science, and an art, of compassion" whose highest goal should be "saving lives above all else." This positions sustainable interaction design not merely as an environmental concern but as a humanitarian imperative.

## 8. Discussion Questions

1. **Balancing mitigation and adaptation:** The authors argue that interaction designers should pursue both emissions reduction and climate adaptation. Given limited resources in any design project, how should designers prioritize between these two goals? What criteria might help determine when adaptation-focused design is more appropriate than mitigation-focused design?

2. **Scientific uncertainty and design decisions:** The paper acknowledges significant disagreement among climate scientists (Hansen vs. Dyson, IPCC vs. Lovelock) about timing and severity of climate impacts. How should interaction designers handle scientific uncertainty when making design decisions? Is the "hope for the best, prepare for the worst" framework sufficient guidance, or does it risk misallocating design resources?

3. **The role of consciousness-raising:** Blevis and Blevis argue that "consciousness raising is our only hope" to address climate change. What are the limitations of consciousness-raising as a design strategy? Given research on the attitude-behavior gap (people often don't act on their environmental knowledge), what additional design strategies might be needed beyond information provision?

4. **Ethics of "saving life" as design goal:** The authors provocatively state that "resigning to Lovelock's prediction of the potential loss of roughly six out of every seven people and many species is not an option" and that interaction design should prioritize "saving lives above all else." What ethical frameworks support or challenge this position? How does this relate to current debates about design's responsibilities in other crisis contexts?

## 9. Bias Check

**Reflection on Neutrality:**

This article is explicitly normative and advocacy-oriented rather than neutral or purely descriptive. The authors take clear positions on climate change (it is real, urgent, and requires action) and on the responsibilities of interaction designers (they should address it through both mitigation and adaptation). This is appropriate for a forum article in *interactions*, which is intended to advance positions within the field.

Several potential biases should be noted:

1. **Selection bias in scientific sources:** The authors rely heavily on James Lovelock's most pessimistic predictions while acknowledging they are not qualified to evaluate competing scientific claims. This may overstate certainty about worst-case scenarios.

2. **Disciplinary advocacy:** As sustainable HCI researchers, the authors have professional interests in arguing that interaction design is uniquely positioned to address climate change.

3. **Western/developed-world perspective:** The examples and assumptions largely reflect concerns of technologically advanced societies, though climate impacts disproportionately affect developing nations.

4. **Optimism about design's power:** The paper assumes that well-designed interactive systems can meaningfully influence both individual behaviors and collective outcomes, which may overestimate design's social impact.

5. **2010 temporal context:** The paper reflects 2010 climate science and HCI discourse; some predictions and framings have evolved significantly since publication.

**Accuracy Score: 7/10**

The paper accurately represents the climate science of its era and the state of sustainable HCI discourse in 2010. However, it is a conceptual/argumentative piece rather than empirical research, so "accuracy" relates more to faithful representation of sources than to verified findings. The authors are transparent about their normative stance and the speculative nature of their design proposals. Points deducted for heavy reliance on Lovelock's controversial extreme predictions and for presenting design interventions without empirical validation of their effectiveness.
